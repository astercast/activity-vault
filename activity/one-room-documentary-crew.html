One-Room Documentary Crew

On a rainy day, a teen crew films a short documentary about one real room: consent first, interviews on the record, and a cut planned before it is shot.

Ages
13–14
Group
6–18 kids
Time
50–60 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Choose the room before the weather chooses the session, and choose it like documentary makers: the room must have a question in it — how does this kitchen feed forty people, what does a repair morning actually repair, who keeps this library alive — because a film about a room with no question is a tour, and tours are what this record refuses to make. Agree the room with its owners and its regular people in advance, in plain words: who the crew is, what a documentary is, where the finished film will be shown (in the room, to the program, and nowhere else without fresh consent), and that any person in the room may decline to be filmed or interviewed, at any point, including after the fact, and have their material deleted in front of them if they ask. Write the film's question and the first shot plan yourself with the crew's officers before the session — plan, not script: documentary shoots a real hour, and its plan is a list of honest intentions (the room waking up, the work's hardest moment, one interview per role) that the real hour is allowed to rearrange. Test the camera in the room's real light and sound at setup: a room that sounds like a tiled cave on the test clip will sound like one in the film, and positions, not hope, fix that.

Materials

  • one room worth filming, chosen with its owners' agreement before the rain day is spent (a workshop, a kitchen in its working hour, a library corner, the program's own store room — a room with a real life of work, people who belong to it, and something that changes across the session the crew can honestly follow)
  • one camera, adult-managed (a phone or camera on a tripod or a steady rest, charged, with space cleared and tested at setup — the crew's only machine, operated under the adult's eye, with the adult holding the device and its files before, during and after the session)
  • the consent cards and the shot plan (a card per person filmed or interviewed: what the film is, where it will be shown, and their signed yes or their no, which is final and needs no reason — and the shot plan, one page: the film's question, its planned shots in order, its interviews, agreed before the camera rolls)
  • the crew log (one page per session: shots taken against the plan, interviews and their consents, anything declined and how the plan absorbed it, and the edit order the crew agreed — kept by the crew's keeper at the camera's side)

Steps

  1. The framing, camera still capped, the room's owners present or their agreement read aloud: 'Consent comes before camera, every time. Today we make a short documentary — a true film about a true room and the people whose work it is. True is the job. We do not stage moments and call them found, we do not film anyone who has not said yes on a card, we do not keep footage of anyone who changes their mind, and we do not make anybody look foolish for an audience's easy laugh, because the people in this room will watch this film in this room, and a crew that would not show its cut to its subjects has made the wrong cut. Our plan is on one page: the question, the shots, the interviews. The room's real hour will rearrange it — that is documentary happening, not the plan failing — and the log will record what we actually got and what we honestly missed. Nobody performs. Nobody is ambushed. The camera is the last thing that starts and the first thing that stops when anyone says stop.' Consent cards are offered and collected before the cap comes off, declines are entered in the log with the same respect as consents, and the shot plan is amended in the open around them.
  2. Shooting the room's hour, the crew at its stations: the crew works the plan in its published roles — camera, sound-watcher with the test clip's lessons in their ears, interviewer, and keeper logging every shot against the plan with its time and its honest one-line verdict. Shots are taken from the positions setup proved, the camera rests steady rather than wandering, and the crew's discipline is duration: hold a shot past the interesting moment, because rooms speak in their ordinary minutes and a crew that only grabs highlights will assemble a film about grabbing. Nobody directs the room's people to repeat an action for the camera and call it documentary — if a moment matters and was missed, the log records it missed, and the interview may ask about it, which is the honest way a film recovers a moment. The keeper calls the plan's state at the session's middle — shots held, shots lost, interviews owed — and the crew re-plans its remaining time in the open, cutting planned shots by the log's evidence rather than by panic.
  3. The interviews, conducted as the framing promised: each interviewee sits where they work, the question list is the one the plan published — the film's question from their chair, the hardest part of the work, the thing visitors never see — and follow-ups may only chase what the person has just said, never ambush them toward a line the crew wishes it had. The interviewer states at the start of every interview, on the recording, who is filming, what for, and that the person may stop, strike an answer, or withdraw entire after the fact — consent is re-spoken at the microphone, not just signed at the door, because a card signed an hour ago should not have to do a person's remembering for them. Interruptions, hesitations and the room's own sounds stay in the material: the crew is filming a room, not a studio, and its honesty includes the kettle. An interview that runs dry is ended kindly and early by the interviewer, thanked, and logged as it was; the film owes nobody a performance, least of all its subjects.
  4. The edit order, agreed at the table before anyone touches a timeline: the crew watches or hears its material entire, keeper's log in hand, and argues the film's shape from evidence — which shots actually hold the question, which interview line the whole film leans on, what the opening image promises and whether the ending pays it. The cut is planned on paper first: shots in order, lengths in honest estimates, interview extracts chosen by what was said and not by who said it most fluently, and any person who struck or withdrew material is honoured in the plan before a single frame is placed, their absence shaped around with respect rather than disguised. Where the site edits on the day, the adult drives the machine and the crew directs; where it does not, the paper cut is the session's finished work and says so — a complete, argued edit plan is a real documentary outcome, and the log records which the session achieved and why. Titles and credits are agreed with the room's people: names as they wish them written, roles as they actually are.
  5. Closing the session, the film or the plan shown first to its subjects: the crew's standing law is that the room sees it before any other audience — a rough cut screened in the filmed room, or the paper cut read aloud there shot by shot — and corrections of fact from the room's people are entered in the log and honoured in the final shape, because a documentary about people that its people call wrong is wrong, whatever it cost to shoot. The tell-back is said in the filmed room, camera capped and put away: 'This morning this room was just where the work happens. It is a film now, or a plan for one that a stranger could finish: a question asked honestly, an hour shot without staging, interviews given freely and strikable freely, and a cut argued from what we actually got. Consent came before camera, every time, and the room saw the result before anyone else did. That is what documentary means when teenagers make it properly: true, permitted, and answerable to its subjects.' The device and its files go to the adult's keeping with the log's inventory of what exists, what was deleted at whose request, and where the finished film may be shown — the crew's last entry, made before anyone leaves the room.

Variations

Easier: A silent-film version (a room whose sound defeats recording, or a first crew): the whole discipline shot as a visual documentary — planned shots, held durations, title cards written by the crew to carry the question — with interviews replaced by observed work and the consent laws entire.

Harder: A rain-only version (the room is the weather's own): the crew films the rain itself through and on the building — gutters, windows, the yard's new rivers — from inside the agreed positions, a documentary with no interviewees, where the consent laws rest and the shot plan and the log carry the whole discipline.

⚠️ Safety

Consent and safeguarding set this record's laws and they outrank every shot: nothing is posted, shared or uploaded from the session — no stills, no clips, no behind-the-scenes photographs — by anyone, and the finished film is shown only where the consent cards said it would be shown, to the audiences they named. The device is the adult's charge throughout: it enters the room with the adult, its files are inventoried in the log, material struck or withdrawn by any subject is deleted under the adult's hand with the deletion entered in the log, and no crew member copies material to a personal device for any reason, including enthusiasm. Filming never follows anyone into private spaces — no changing areas, no toilets, no offices with closed doors — and a person entering the room who has not consented is not filmed: the camera turns away or stops, in the moment, without the moment becoming a discussion. Interviews follow the site's safeguarding shape: in the open room, within the adult's sight, at the subject's workplace, and any disclosure a subject makes that raises a concern is the adult's to act on through the site's own channels — the crew's job is to stop filming and fetch the adult, and the framing says so in those words. Equipment safety is ordinary and kept: tripod legs taped or watched in walkways, cables out of paths, no filming while walking backwards, ever — the camera's operator stands still or is guided by the sound-watcher's hand on their shoulder, because a room's real hour includes real thresholds and real kettles.

🤝 Inclusion notes

A film crew is a row of different crafts at full standing, and this record staffs it as such: the keeper — consents, shot log, the plan's living state, the calls at the middle — runs the shoot from the camera's side, and the interviewer's chair, the session's voice of honour, is placed where its holder works best, with questions carried on printed cards that can be read, shown, or handed to a co-interviewer to voice at the holder's direction. Camera work can be tripod-based entire — framing, holding and calling shots from a seated position at the room's agreed vantage — and sound-watching, the crew's most sensitive craft, is headphone work at the test clip's positions, open to a member whose gift is listening rather than looking. Shot-planning and the edit argument are table work where every member's evidence counts the same: the log's verdicts, not the loudest recollection, decide what the film holds. Consent is designed for real humans: cards in large clear print, read aloud on request, a decline that can be given by word, gesture or simply stepping out of the marked filming area — the room's floor plan marks a no-camera refuge corner, ordinary furniture rather than exile, so stepping out of the film never means stepping out of the room.
