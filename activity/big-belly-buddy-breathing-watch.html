Big Belly Buddy Breathing Watch

Lie down, set one small soft buddy on your belly, and watch it ride: up when the breath comes in, down when the breath goes out. That is the whole watch.

Before you begin

Ages
3–3
Group
2–10 kids
Time
10–15 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
No prep needed

Setup

Choose the buddies like casting: each must be light (a heavy buddy argues with a small breath and the breath loses), soft (it will be handled, hugged, and occasionally dropped on a face, kindly), and just big enough that its riding shows from the watcher's own chin-down view. That view, down one's own front, is the session's whole cinema, and the adult tests it lying on a mat with the day's heaviest candidate before anyone else lies down. Breathe, once, and watch your own buddy ride: if you cannot see it move, the room's smaller breathers cannot either, and the buddy is recast. Lay the mats with honest space between them. A breathing room is a room where nobody's elbow is in anybody else's weather.

Materials

  • one small soft buddy per watcher (a palm-sized soft toy or bean-filled animal. Light enough to ride a belly, big enough to see riding; buddies are chosen by their watchers, because a buddy is a relationship)
  • mats or a carpet soft enough for lying (the shore of the session. One per watcher, with a body's worth of space on every side)

Steps

  1. Lay the watchers down, one mat each, buddies held, and say the plain framing: 'Your breath is moving all day and you never get to watch it. Today you do. Lie back. Put your buddy on your belly. There. Now just breathe like you always breathe, and watch: when the breath comes IN, your belly lifts, and your buddy rides UP. When the breath goes OUT, down it comes. Your only job is watching. The breathing does itself.' State the laws whole: 'Buddies ride bellies. They are not thrown, and they do not visit anybody else's belly. Hands rest beside you or hold your buddy steady at the start; then hands rest too. Anybody may sit up and be finished, any time, quietly. Resting is allowed to end.'
  2. First ride together, everyone's at once: the adult lies on the middle mat where the room can glance sideways and copy, buddy placed, and breathes at honest size, no performance breath, no great heaving, narrating the first two rides in the session's official shape, quiet as snowfall: 'In... up it rides. Out... down it comes. In... up. Out... down. That is my breath, doing what it does all day, and my buddy is showing me.' Then the narration stops, and the room watches its own buddies in the first true quiet of the session, which the adult protects by doing the session's hardest work: nothing, visibly, at full length.
  3. Slow-bell stretch, once the riding is familiar: the adult's voice returns, once, to offer the session's one deepening: 'See if your buddy can ride SLOWER. Not bigger, slower. The breath can take its time going out, like a lift coming down gently.' Slow rides spread round the room the way yawns do, honestly and by contagion, and the reports, when the sitting-up comes, collect in the session's tiny official shape: 'Mine went slow at the end.' 'Mine fell off twice and I put it back.' A fallen buddy is the record's expected guest, never an error: it is replaced by its own watcher, without sitting up if they can manage it, with sitting up if they can't, and the watch resumes. The replacing is part of the watching, and the record says so if a face falls with the buddy.
  4. Sleeping-buddy finish, the session's close: the adult tells the room the buddies are tired from all their riding and are going to be rocked the last of the way, watchers slow their watching to the session's final minute, breaths at whatever size they have honestly become, buddies riding small and steady, and the adult's closing words come at the volume of the room itself by then: 'Still riding. Up... and down. Your breath carried your buddy the whole watch and never once asked your help. It will carry you all day, too. It is doing it now.' Watchers sit up SLOWLY (a watched breath makes a dreamy head; the record knows and says so), buddies hugged once and retired to the buddy basket by their own watchers.
  5. Tell-back, said over the basket before the room stands: 'Nineteen buddies rode, and every rider was a breath nobody had to be taught. Slow at the end, all of them. Your breath can do that whenever the day gets loud. You know where it lives now, and your buddy can show you any time you lie down together.' Mats are rolled by their watchers, two hands and a knee, badly and proudly, because the session's calm survives the tidying and should.

Variations

Easier: A five-minute version: mats, buddies, three narrated rides and a quiet minute, the whole watch, pocket-sized, for a loud moment in a long day.

Harder: A story-buddy version: the buddy that rides is the class's story bear, passed mat to mat across the week, every watcher rides the SAME buddy, and its week's worth of rides is the class's shared weather report.

Safety

Nothing in this record is medical and nothing is promised: it is watching your own breath move a soft toy, described as exactly that, and any child who finds lying still uncomfortable, for any reason, named or not, sits up, takes a chair at the mats' edge, and watches a buddy ride on the adult's belly at full honour, because the watching is the record and it works from a chair. Buddies are palm-sized, soft, and light, checked for loose seams and fillings before the session, because a buddy will be hugged, dropped, and investigated; anything small enough to mouth never joins the cast. Mats are spaced so no rolling watcher meets a neighbour, buddies are never thrown across the room (a thrown buddy ends its flight in the adult's hands and rejoins at the next session), and the sitting-up at the end is slow by spoken instruction. Dreamy heads and fast standing are a wobble the record designs out. The room's door stays open and the session is short: quiet that is chosen, bounded, and endable by sitting up is the only kind this record keeps.

Inclusion notes

The watch fits every watcher: the buddy can ride a belly, a chest, or a tray resting across a seated watcher's lap, and it can be watched from lying, from reclining against cushions, or from a chair where the adult's own buddy performs the riding at narration distance. The in-and-up belongs to whoever's breath is watched or whoever is watching, and both are full citizenship. A watcher can run the session's one instrument, the narrating voice for the first rides ('In... up it rides'), which is the quiet-room honour and rotates by list across the week's sessions. Reports arrive as the tiny sentences, a slow hand rising and falling in imitation, the room's favourite report of all, a sign, or a buddy held up and tipped in demonstration; every report joins the tell-back in the same shape.
