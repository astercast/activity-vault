Big Cloud Lying, Picture Names

One big blanket on the grass, the whole group on its back, eyes up: every cloud is being something today, and the group's job is to say what.

Before you begin

Ages
3–3
Group
3–12 kids
Time
10–15 min
Where
Outdoors
Prep
No prep needed

Setup

Do the looking first, alone, standing where the blanket will go: if the sky is one clean blue, this record waits for a cloudier morning and says so, a gallery with no pictures in it teaches pointing at nothing, and the sky provides so often that honesty costs a day at most. Check the grass the way a back would meet it: walk the blanket's patch in your own socks if you doubt it, feeling for stones, sticks, and the wet that soaks through in a minute. Lie down yourself for ten seconds in the middle of the session's future and look up: you are checking sightlines (the group must see sky, not the fence's top) and the sun's position. The lying spot is chosen so the sun is never in a lying face, because the record's one gaze goes UP.

Materials

  • one big blanket or old sheet, grass-sized (the lying blanket, spread by the adult on grass checked by hand and by eye: dry-ish, flat, and free of anything a back should not meet)
  • a sky with clouds in it (the session's whole gallery. The adult has looked up before gathering anyone: a cloudless sky postpones the record, honestly, and the group is told why)

Steps

  1. Spread the blanket, walk the group out around it, and say the plain framing: 'Clouds are not just clouds. Look up long enough and every one of them is being SOMETHING, a ship, a sheep, a sleeping dog, and today we are lying down to catch them at it. Each person names what one cloud is being, in their own words, and the whole group looks where the naming points.' State the laws whole: 'We lie on the blanket, on our backs, and we stay lying while the naming goes on. Sitting up ends your looking, kindly understood. Pointing is done with a whole arm, slowly: the sky is big and a fast point loses everybody. The sun is never looked at. Our clouds live away from it today, and a face that finds the sun in its view turns to the blanket's shadier end.'
  2. First naming together, yours: lie down in the middle of the blanket with the group settling round you like petals, take a full breath of just looking, the record insists on the breath, because a sky named too fast is a sky unseen, and give the first name in the session's official shape, pointing the whole arm up at one honest cloud: 'That one is being... a WHALE. There is its back. There is its tail, going.' Narrate only what the cloud truly offers; a name that needs five invented features is a name the cloud didn't earn, and the room can tell. The group looks where you point until heads nod: seeing what a namer sees, together, is the skill, and it arrives in this minute or it doesn't arrive at all.
  3. Naming round: namers take the sky in known order, each choosing one cloud, by pointing the whole arm and holding it until the group's eyes arrive, and naming what it is being, in the official shape, with the adult as the room's pointer-reader: 'Mina's cloud, up from her hand, past the tree, THERE, the long one. A TRAIN, she says. I can see its carriages.' A name received is repeated by the adult exactly as given, never improved: 'a boat with a hat' stands in the record as said, because it is almost certainly true. Clouds nobody claims are named by the sky itself, via the adult's wondering aloud: 'And that small one, all alone. Nobody's named it. It might be being a secret.'
  4. Cloud-changing stretch, for a group whose looking lasts: return to the FIRST cloud named, the whale, and look at what it is being NOW: 'It was a whale. Its tail has gone somewhere. Now its back is longer and its head has... melted into a hill.' The lesson lands in the open, at the record's own pace: clouds do not hold their shapes for anybody, a name is true for the minute it is said in, and watching a picture un-become is as much looking as watching it arrive. A namer whose cloud dissolves entirely mid-round is honoured, not pitied: 'Your cloud finished being a bear and went back to being weather. You caught the whole of it. Nobody else can say that.'
  5. Finish with one whole-group cloud, chosen by the adult's slow arm sweeping to the biggest cloud in reach of everybody's eyes, named by the circle in chorus, three names offered, the group settling on one by the ancient method of which feels most true said together, and one full breath of looking at it, all together, in quiet. Then the sitting-up, done slowly (the sky spins a little after long looking; the record knows, and says so: 'Up slowly. The ground has to catch up with your eyes'), the blanket shaken out by many hands, and the tell-back said under the sky that hosted it: 'Seven clouds were something today, and we caught them being it. They have all gone back to being weather now. The sky will make more. It always does.'

Variations

Easier: A three-cloud version: three namers, three clouds, the breath and the pointing whole, inside ten minutes on a busy morning.

Harder: A window version (a wet week, a room with big glass): the same lying, naming, and pointing done on rugs at the window's base, heads on cushions. The gallery is the same sky, the frame is glass.

Safety

Eyes are the record's instruments and the sun is its one hazard: the blanket is laid where faces look up into cloud country away from the sun, the adult checks the sun's arc before the session and moves the blanket rather than arguing with it, and any face that finds glare turns to the blanket's shade end or takes a hat's brim as its instrument. Looking is done lying down, nobody walks with their eyes up, nobody stands on the blanket, and the sitting-up at the end is slow by instruction because a sky-watched head is a dizzy head for ten honest seconds. The grass patch is adult-checked for stones, sticks, and stingers' country before the blanket lands; anyone may be a watcher from a chair at the blanket's edge at full honour if the ground is not for them today. The session is short by design: fifteen minutes of sky is a feast at three, and the record ends while the looking is still sweet.

Inclusion notes

Naming is the record's door and it stands open at every height: a namer can name from the blanket, from a chair at its edge with the same sky overhead, or from a lap, and can name by pointing a whole arm, by describing ('the one over the fence corner, the LONG one'), or by choosing among the adult's offered sightings ('your cloud, is it the one being a ship?') with the name itself still the namer's own word, kept exact. The pointer-reader job, repeating each name at full size so the whole blanket hears it land, is the session's official voice and belongs to whoever holds it, including a namer who reads for others between their own turns. A name signed, tapped out on a friend's arm in agreed rhythm, or sung is a name; the record's gallery collects them all in the same frame.
