Big Paper Fan Wind Maker

One big stiff paper fan per wind maker: flap it slow, flap it fast, and feel the wind you made arrive on your own face — weather, by hand.

Ages
3–3
Group
3–12 kids
Time
10–15 min
Where
Outdoors & Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Make the fans yourself and flap-test every one: the fan must move real air at a three-year-old's flap — stiff enough to push weather, light enough to push it fifty times. A fan that folds, flops, or sheds its grip in your hands will do it in theirs, mid-ceremony, so tape grips twice and retire any fan whose folds soften. Practise the two speeds the session names until your hands know them without thinking: SLOW WEATHER (one flap, a pause, the air arriving like a sigh) and FAST WEATHER (flaps in a run, the streamer standing out). This record is taught by a body that has felt both, or it is not taught at all.

Materials

  • big paper fans, adult-made (one per maker: a sheet of stiff card folded accordion-style and taped firmly at the grip, or a paper plate on a wide craft stick — blunt, light, and strong enough that a dropped fan is a noise, never a hazard)
  • one very light streamer or scarf on a stick, adult-held (the wind teller — it shows the wind the makers cannot see)
  • a shady standing spot the adult has chosen (the weather station)

Steps

  1. Gather the makers at the weather station and say the plain framing: 'Wind is moving air. Usually the sky makes it. Today YOU make it. A fan, flapped, pushes the air, and the air travels and lands on a face — that landing is the wind arriving.' Hold the wind teller where all can see it. State the laws whole: 'Fans flap at the air in front of you, never at anybody's face point-blank, and never touching anybody. A fan is held in two hands when it is not flapping. When I call STILL AIR, every fan rests — the wind stops when the makers say so.'
  2. First wind together, yours: flap SLOW WEATHER at the wind teller and let the group watch the streamer lift, lean, and settle — the invisible made visible in the session's first minute. Then turn your fan on your own face, eyes closed, and report in the session's official shape: 'Slow weather. It arrived gently. It took a moment to get here.' That delay — push, travel, arrive — is the record's whole science, and it is felt on the adult's face before it is asked of anyone's.
  3. Making round: makers take their flaps in known order — first slow weather at the teller, reported by the group ('The streamer leaned a LITTLE'); then a run of fast weather, reported at full size ('It STOOD OUT — that was FAST WEATHER') — then, the round's real treasure, wind made for a partner: pairs stand an arm-and-a-half apart, one flaps slow weather, the other receives with eyes open or closed by their own choice, and reports the arrival in the official shape: 'It came. It was gentle. It took a moment.' Makers swap; receivers become weather. Nobody flaps at a partner who has not agreed to be rained on by air.
  4. Still-air stretch, the session's deep half: the whole group flaps together — slow, then fast, the streamer dancing — and on the adult's call, STILL AIR: every fan rests at once, and the group stands in the sudden quiet of weather they have stopped, feeling the last of their own wind arrive and finish. Hold the stillness a full breath. Say the line the record keeps for exactly here: 'We made weather, and then we STOPPED it. The sky cannot do that. You can.'
  5. Finish with the fans retired in order: each maker closes their fan (folds together, grip down) and lays it in the weather box, counted aloud to its true number, while the tell-back is said over the box: 'Slow weather leaned the streamer. Fast weather stood it straight out. And the still air at the end was ours too. The wind went home in a box, and it will come out whenever this group says so.'

Variations

Easier: A three-maker version: three fans, the teller, and both speeds — the whole science inside ten minutes on a still morning.

Harder: A hot-day version: the making round is run entirely in partner pairs at receiving distance — wind made as a gift on a day when a gift of moving air is genuinely worth having, each maker serving two partners before receiving.

⚠️ Safety

Fans are blunt, light, and checked before every session; a fan whose folds tear or whose grip tape lifts is retired on the spot, because a flapping thing coming apart invites grabbing hands at face height. Flapping happens at arm's length from every face — a maker's own or a partner's: partners stand an arm-and-a-half apart, the receiver's agreement is asked and waited for each time, and a receiver may end their weather instantly with a raised hand, honoured without a word of persuasion. Fans are never swung overhead, thrown, or run with; makers walk to their stations with fans held closed in two hands. On a genuinely windy day the record changes its claim honestly — the sky is already making weather — and the session becomes a comparison ('ours vs the sky's') rather than a contest with it.

🤝 Inclusion notes

Wind-making works from any station: a fan can be worked one-handed at whatever arc a maker's arm prefers, clamped open on a tray edge and pushed in strokes, or directed — 'slow... slower... NOW fast' — with a partner's hands following the call while the speeds, the timing, and the still-air call stay the director's. Receiving is equal citizenship: a receiver who reports by a raised thumb, a sign, or the agreed sound for 'it arrived' has reported in full. The wind teller is held by the session's official witness, who calls the streamer's verdicts for the room — 'leaned a little... STOOD OUT' — and that voice belongs to whoever holds it, from any seat at the station.
