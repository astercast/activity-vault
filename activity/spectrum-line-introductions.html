Spectrum Line Introductions

New teens answer screened, low-stakes statements with their feet first. Stand somewhere on the line, then talk to the person furthest from your spot.

Before you begin

Ages
13–14
Group
8–30 kids
Time
30–45 min
Where
Indoors & Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Screen the deck before you mark the line, and screen it harder than feels necessary: the host and the adult read every statement aloud, stand where they honestly would, and ask the screening's one question. Could any person in this room be hurt, exposed or singled out by standing honestly on this one? Statements about family, money, bodies, belief, romance, ability and anyone's private life fail that question by category, not by wording, and they never reach the deck however gently phrased. What survives is the deck's honest territory: mornings versus nights, sweet versus salty, doing versus watching, the best season, planning versus improvising. Tastes a room can disagree about at full volume and no cost. Lay the line for the room that exists: along the longest clear run the space offers, step-free along its whole length, the edge positions beyond both ends laid with the same care as the line itself, and walking room behind the line so people can reach a far position without climbing through the sorted. Set the session's shape in advance: the line taught with two practice statements, talked rounds between statements, the deck's order mixed so no two heavy disagreements stand back to back.

Materials

  • one long line marked on the floor or ground, end to end, that the whole group can stand along at once (tape, a rope laid straight, or a row of cones, long enough that thirty people sorting themselves along it have room to be exact, with its two ends labelled STRONGLY and NOT AT ALL, or the pair the statement needs, in letters the whole line can read)
  • the statement deck, screened in advance by the adult and the host together (low-stakes, answerable, kind statements about tastes, habits and harmless preferences. Every statement read aloud twice at the screening and tested against the deck's law: nobody should be able to be embarrassed by where they stand)
  • the edge cards (two cards or marked mats beyond each end of the line, labelled PASS and NOT SURE, full positions with full standing, part of the line's geography from the first statement, never an apology added later)
  • the host's slate (the session's light record: statements used, pairings made across the line, and any statement the room found flat or surprisingly divisive, entered by the session's keeper for the deck's next screening)

Steps

  1. The framing, the whole group standing behind the unmarked side of the line, deck in the host's hand: 'Your feet answer first; then you get to explain. I will read a statement. You will walk to the place on this line that is true for you. Strongly at this end, not at all at that end, and every honest place between, including the exact middle, which is an answer and not a failure to answer. Then, before anyone explains anything to the room, you will talk for two minutes, not to your neighbour, who mostly agrees with you, but across the line: find the person standing furthest from your spot, go to them, and each explain, in one sentence each, what put you where you are standing. You are not there to win them over. You are there to be able to say, at the end, what the room looks like from the other end of one question. The edge positions are part of the line: PASS means this one is not for you today, NOT SURE means exactly that, and both are stood in with the same standing as anywhere else. Nobody will ask you to account for either. Nobody's place is recorded by name, nobody is photographed standing anywhere, and a statement you dislike can simply be watched from the edge at full membership. Feet first. Talking second. Winning never.' Two practice statements are run entire, walk, pair across, one sentence each, return, so the room learns the shape with its feet before it trusts the shape with its opinions.
  2. The standing rounds, the session's working rhythm: the host reads each statement twice, slowly, and the room sorts itself in its own time, no countdown, no last-person spotlight, because a line that shames its slowest sorter has stopped being an introduction and started being a race. The host reads the sorted line aloud in the neutral language the framing promised: where the room bunched, where it split cleanly, where the middle carried the day, and any cluster named by position, never by person. 'The strong end is crowded on this one' is the whole commentary a bunch earns. Surprises are the deck's gold and the host marks them without exploiting them: a statement the screening expected to split the room that lands nearly unanimous, or a harmless taste that cleaves the room in two, is entered on the slate for the deck's next screening, because a deck, like a crew, improves by being re-screened against evidence. Between standing rounds the room resets fully behind the line. A sorted line that never dissolves teaches people to defend a spot instead of answering the next question fresh.
  3. The talking rounds, where introductions actually happen: on the host's call, each person finds the furthest standing person from their own position, the host pairs any remainder, including themselves in, so nobody is left holding their sentence with nowhere to take it, and the pairs take their two minutes where they stand or a step off the line, by the pair's own agreement. The law of the two minutes is stated once and kept by courtesy rather than policing: one sentence of explanation each first, entire and uninterrupted, what put you there, in the plainest words you have, then open talk for the remainder, which is where names, programs, common ground and the session's actual introductions are exchanged, at standing distance from the disagreement that caused them. A pair that finishes early does not drift back to its own end's comfort; the host's standing invitation is to stay and keep talking until the call, because the furthest pairing is the session's whole design and its minutes are the minutes that work. Pairs dissolve on the call without verdicts: nobody reports back, nobody is quoted to the room, and a sentence heard in a pair stays in the pair unless its speaker chooses to offer it again themselves.
  4. The deck's deepening, used only when the room is warm and the line is trusted: the host may, twice in a session and no more, follow a statement with its one allowed sequel, 'stand where you stood a year ago', and the room re-sorts itself along the same line to its remembered position, and looks, in silence for a counted ten, at how far its members have travelled in both directions. The sequel works because it is rare, because it is about change rather than difference, and because the furthest pairing that follows it talks about journeys instead of positions; a host who reaches for it a third time is spending the room's trust to fill the session, and the slate records sequels used so the next host inherits the discipline with the deck. Statements may also be proposed by the room late in the session, and they enter the deck only through the same screening law, read aloud and tested on the spot by the host and the adult together, in the room's hearing. A proposed statement that fails is declined with its reason stated kindly and briefly, which teaches the screening better than any lecture.
  5. Closing, the line unmarked and the slate read back: the final statement is the deck's gentlest by design, the screening saves one the room will mostly share, so the session's last picture of itself is a bunch, laughing at its own unanimity, then the pairs talk, dissolve, and the group gathers off the line entirely while the host reads the slate: statements used, the surprises, the sequels spent, and the pairings' count, with no person's position on any statement named, recorded or recalled. The tell-back is said standing where the line was, its tape still down: 'An hour ago this room was thirty strangers standing behind a line. You have sorted yourselves, honestly, a dozen times since, bunched, split, stranded at ends and crowded in middles, and every time, you walked the length of your disagreement to hear one sentence from the person furthest from your spot, and gave them yours. Nobody won a single statement. Everybody can now say what the room looks like from somewhere they do not stand. That is an introduction with the geography left in. Feet first, talking second, winning never.' The line is lifted by its ends' keepers, the deck and slate are stored together, and any statement the slate marked is re-screened before the deck is used again.

Variations

Easier: A seated-line version (a small room or a settled group): the line runs along a table or a row of chairs, positions are taken by standing a card at a place along it, and the furthest pairings talk across the table, the geography, the screening and the laws entire, at table scale.

Harder: A short-deck version (twenty minutes, a first meeting): six statements, practice pair included, no sequels, the whole discipline in its shortest honest form, with the closing slate and the tell-back kept entire because they are where the session's manners live.

Safety

The deck is this record's safety system and its screening law is absolute: statements are screened in advance against the categories named at setup, no statement is ever improvised by the host mid-session to chase a laugh, and a statement that lands badly, read by the room's silence, a visible flinch along the line, laughter at a position rather than with the room, is retired on the spot by the host, kindly and without analysis, and entered on the slate as retired with its reason, because a deck improves by amputation as well as addition. Nobody's position is photographed, filmed, recorded by name or reported outside the room, and the host states that law at the framing where the whole line can hear it. The line's geography is walked at setup: step-free along its whole run, no standing on furniture to be seen, the furthest pairing reached by walking behind the line and never through a sorted crowd at a run, and the session's pace is set so sorting is a walk, always. A person may stand at the edge, sit the session at the line's side as keeper, or leave and return without any accounting. The host's attention to a quiet edge-stander is one neutral check-in, offered once, and a no is a complete answer the session honours by moving on with genuine warmth.

Inclusion notes

The line was designed so that standing anywhere on it, including off it, is a full position, and the inclusion is structural rather than appended: the PASS and NOT SURE edges are laid, labelled and named in the framing with the same ceremony as the ends, a middle position is introduced as an answer in its own words, and the keeper's office at the line's side, slate, pairings, the deck's memory, is a seated role with the session's whole record in its hands. Sorting can be done at any pace and by any route: a member may take the long way behind the line, place themselves by telling the keeper their position and having a marker stood there by a volunteer at their direction, or hold a position card at the line's side in a place that corresponds to their answer. The geography is the game's, the access to it is the person's. Pairings talk at standing, seated or leaning distance as the pair agrees, the one-sentence law can be met in speech, in writing shown to the partner, or by a gesture vocabulary the pair settles in its first ten seconds, and no pairing is ever reported to the room, so nobody's explanation needs to survive an audience. Statements are read twice, slowly, and shown on a card at the line's middle for anyone who takes language in better by eye; the host faces the line while reading, every time, as a matter of the session's ordinary manners.
