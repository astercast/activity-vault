Dusk Bat Exit Count

Teens count bats leaving a known roost at dusk from one set watch point: sectors, silence, tallies by the minute, and no bat ever approached or handled.

Ages 13–14 · 45–60 min · 4 materials

Ages
13–14
Group
4–14 kids
Time
45–60 min
Where
Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Confirm the roost before you promise the count anything, and confirm it through people who know: the adults contact the roost's owner or the local bat group, establish that the roost is active this season, agree the watch point's distance and the count's dates, and learn the local rules that protect the animals, in many places bats and their roosts are protected by law, and the count operates inside those rules by design, not by exception. Visit the watch point in daylight with the adults: walk the route in and out, find the firmest ground, settle where the crew will sit, where bags go, and how a watcher who needs to leave is escorted out without crossing the exit's skyline. Set the count's shape now and write it on the book's first page as law: arrival before sunset, silence from the first-call time, sectors assigned, tallies by the minute, and the end call given by the keeper when the exit has truly finished, not when the crew is cold. Check the season and the forecast honestly: an exit count belongs to the bats' active season and to a dry, mild evening, and a count postponed for weather is a count protected, with the postponement entered in the book as a decision, not a failure.

Materials

  • a known, established roost the adults have confirmed with its owner or a local bat group in advance (a roost whose location may be watched from outside, at a distance the guidance sets. The count never reveals a roost's location to anyone outside the crew and its hosts)
  • one set watch point, chosen and marked before the count night (a spot with a clear silhouette view of the exit against the evening sky, far enough away that the watchers are furniture, not a threat. The point is paced out, marked, and drawn on the count sheet)
  • the count sheets and the tally book (one sheet per watcher per sector: minute rows, tally columns, weather, light and the count's start and end calls. Kept in a hardback book that is the roost's whole record across the season)
  • counters and quiet kit (hand tally counters where the site keeps them, clipboards and pencils that work in failing light, and a red-filtered torch held by the adult alone, used only for the walk out, never shone at the roost, ever)

Steps

  1. The framing, at the watch point, light still good, voices already low: 'Count what leaves; never chase what returns. Tonight this roost empties itself into the dusk, one bat at a time and then in ribbons, and our whole job is to be a number at the end of it that somebody can trust. We count from here. We do not approach the roost, we do not shine light at it, we do not handle, feed, follow or film a single animal, and a bat that comes near us is a bat we ignore with discipline, because being ignored by us is what safety feels like to a roost. Each watcher owns one sector of sky. You count only your sector, you tally by the minute, and a bat you are unsure crossed your line is a bat you do not claim, an honest undercount from a careful crew is worth more to the people who study these animals than a heroic guess. When the light goes, the tallies keep the count; nobody needs to see the book until the end call. Settle now. Silence from the first call is the count starting.' Watchers take their sectors, pencils and counters are tested in the remaining light, and the keeper opens the book to the night's sheet.
  2. The count, run by the book's stated calls: the keeper notes sunset, weather and the first bat's minute in the book's own columns before any tally begins, because a count without its conditions cannot be compared with any other night's. From the first call, talk stops. Each watcher tallies their sector's exits minute by minute. A counter click per bat, the minute's total written at the minute's turn where the light allows, or held as clicks and transcribed in the pause the keeper declares if the exit runs hot. Double-counting at sector edges is the count's known error and the crew meets it by the law agreed in daylight: a bat crossing a boundary is counted by the sector it leaves, never by both, and a watcher who loses a ribbon against dark sky marks that minute as lost rather than inventing its number. The book has a column for lost minutes, and using it is a skill, not a confession. The adult watches the crew, not the roost: seated postures, quiet, and the route out kept clear.
  3. The end call and the reckoning, at the watch point while the night finishes settling: the keeper gives the end call only when the exit has been empty for the full agreed stretch, then reads the sheet's conditions back before any total is spoken, sunset, first bat, weather, lost minutes, because a total announced before its conditions is a number already escaping its evidence. Sector tallies are summed aloud, minute by minute where the exit peaked, and the book's night total is agreed between keeper and watchers from the sheets, never from anyone's memory of a spectacular minute. The crew names its surprises honestly, an earlier first bat than the season led them to expect, a sector that carried the whole exit, and its non-surprises with equal respect: a quiet night counted carefully is a finding, and the book says so, because a monitoring record is built from ordinary nights counted well, not from records broken.
  4. The walk out and the count's afterlife, governed as strictly as the count itself: the crew leaves by the daylight-walked route, in the adult's red light, at a walk, in file, with kit counted out by the keeper, counters, sheets, book, and nothing left at the watch point that would tell the ground a crowd had been there. The roost's location is the count's confidence: it is written in the book as the hosts agreed it should be recorded, it is never posted, pinned on a map, shared in a message or pointed out to visitors, and a crew member asked about it has one answer, rehearsed without embarrassment: the count does not say where its roost is. Where the hosts or the bat group keep a monitoring scheme and want the night's sheet, the keeper copies it entire, conditions, lost minutes and all, and the book records to whom it was sent and when, because data that leaves the book should leave it honestly or not at all.
  5. Closing the night, the book finished at the table under real light: the sheets are checked for a name, a sector and a signature on each, the transcribed minutes are re-read against the counters where counters were used, and the crew writes the night's closing page together, total counted, peak minute, first bat against sunset, lost minutes owned, and the one instruction the next count night will need most, earned in the dusk. The tell-back is said over the closed book: 'Tonight a roost emptied into the dark over our heads, and we asked nothing of it but a number. We sat where we said we would sit, we counted only what our sectors honestly held, we marked the minutes we lost instead of inventing them, and we walked out leaving no sign and telling no location. Count what leaves; never chase what returns. That is a count a stranger can build on, and the bats never knew our names.' The book, counters and sheets are stored together where the next count, this crew's or another's. Will find the whole watch in one place.

Variations

Easier: A single-sector version (a small crew or a first count): the whole discipline at one watcher's scale, one sector, one sheet, conditions, lost minutes and the walk out, with the book opened and the laws stated, ready to grow sectors next count.

Harder: A return-count version (a crew in its second season at the same roost): the night's sheet is read against the same dates last season before the crew leaves the table, differences are entered as questions for the hosts or the bat group, never as conclusions of the crew's own, and the book keeps both seasons in the same order.

⚠️ Safety

The animals set this record's first law and it is absolute: no bat is ever handled, fed, approached, lit, filmed at close range or followed, by anyone, for any reason. A grounded or apparently injured bat is never touched by the crew; it is reported to the adult, who contacts the hosts or a licensed bat carer, because bats can carry disease and are protected in many places, and bare-handed rescue is both unlawful and unsafe. Nobody enters any structure, roof space or tree hollow for this count, ever: the watch is outside, at the agreed point, at the agreed distance, and a count whose watch point cannot be held is abandoned, not improvised closer. Dusk is the working light and it is governed: the route in and out is walked in daylight first, the crew moves after dark only in file behind the adult's red light, nobody leaves the watch point alone, and a watcher who feels unwell or unsafe is escorted out by the adult with the count continuing short-handed. The book records the change and the count's honour survives it. Weather ends counts without argument: rain, high wind or a cold snap that would keep the roost home anyway postpones the night, and the postponement is entered as a decision. Insect repellent, where the site uses it, goes on before arrival and well away from the watch point, and hands that have handled it never handle the book's sheets near food afterwards, the crew eats nothing at the watch point at all, which keeps the point clean and the animals unbothered.

🤝 Inclusion notes

A dusk count is seated, silent work by design, and its authority sits exactly there: the book-keeper, seated at the watch point's heart with the sheets, the conditions columns and the calls, runs the count's voice in a whisper economy where the keeper's written signals, agreed in daylight (a raised pencil for the minute's turn, a flat hand for the end call), carry as fully as any voice. Watchers are assigned sectors by the view their seat honestly gives them, tallies can be kept by counter clicks counted by touch, and a watcher whose dusk vision is limited takes the early, bright sector where the exit silhouettes against the strongest sky. The count is designed so every seat has a sector it can hold with honour. Sheets can be transcribed and totals reckoned at the table under full light by members whose night was the book rather than the sky; their arithmetic carries the same ink. The route to the watch point is chosen for the firmest, most step-free approach the site offers, arrival is in full daylight with unhurried settling time, and the crew's size is capped by the point's honest capacity, never by enthusiasm. A quieter count from fewer, well-seated watchers is the better count, and the book says why.
