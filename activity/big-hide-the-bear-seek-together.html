Big Hide the Bear, Seek Together

One big soft bear hides somewhere in the room, not too well. The whole group seeks together, and the finding belongs to everybody.

Before you begin

Ages
3–3
Group
3–14 kids
Time
10–20 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
No prep needed

Setup

Prepare the room, not the bear: walk the hunting ground with a hider's eye and pre-decide the session's three or four honest hiding places, behind the cushion with an ear out, under the low table wearing the rug's shadow, in the book basket with one paw over the edge, places a three-year-old's eye can actually reach, because this record's difficulty lives in the LOOKING, not the hiding, and a bear hidden beyond the room's eyes teaches only waiting. Check each place for the session's one law: the bear is never inside anything that closes, anything heavy, anything with a cord, or anything a seeker must climb to search. Then brief the hider's assistant, you, first, on the ceremony's timing: the group hides its eyes for the count of ten, no more, because at three the covering of eyes is itself an act of faith that must be kept short and kept safe.

Materials

  • one big soft bear (the hider, big enough that a good hiding place still leaves a paw, an ear, or a hopeful nose showing; soft, washed, and famous: this bear has a name and the room knows it)
  • the whole room, arranged as the hunting ground (its ordinary furniture, with the adult's one addition: nothing is hidden IN anything that closes, locks, or tips)

Steps

  1. Gather the group in the room's middle, the bear held up where everyone can greet it, and say the plain framing: 'It is raining, we are inside, and [the bear] would like to play hiding. The bear will hide. Somewhere in this room, where eyes can find it. Nobody hides THEMSELVES: the whole group seeks, together, holding the same search in twenty hands. When somebody spots the bear, they do not grab, they FREEZE, and they point, and the whole group gets the finding.' State the laws whole: 'While the bear hides, our eyes are covered and our bottoms are on the floor, the hiding is a trust, and we keep it. Seeking is walking: hunters walk, in a bunch, where the adult walks. A found bear is lifted by the spotter and carried back like a rescued sailor, at full ceremony.'
  2. First hiding, yours: the group covers its eyes (hands, or faces in laps, every covering is honest), and the adult counts aloud to ten at ceremony pace while hiding the bear in the session's kindest place, an ear and a paw showing, findable by the door-side seekers first. 'Ready or not, eyes open, the bear is... somewhere.' The first search is conducted at the record's official manner: the whole pack moves together, slowly, the adult narrating the search like weather: 'Not behind the door... the bear is not a door bear. The cushions have not been ASKED yet...'. Until the first spot lands (a freeze, a point, a noise like a struck bell) and the room converges on one visible ear: 'FOUND. Mina's eyes found it. Everybody's search found it. The bear was in the cushions, showing one ear the whole time, bravely.' The bear is carried back by its spotter, held high, unhurt by fame.
  3. Seeking round: the hiding passes to its keepers, a child, with the adult as their ladder-free, door-holding assistant, chooses the hiding place from the room's known places (or invents one inside the laws, approved by the adult's eye before the eyes close) while the group covers and counts, the count now given to the circle's steadiest counter. Searches grow craftier in the open: the pack learns to ask places in order, to look LOW (bears are low creatures), to return to the place a paw showed last time, and the adult protects the round's constitution throughout: a bear unfound after one full circuit is not failed but 'winning', and the hider earns the record's highest honour early, revealing it by degrees: 'I will give the room ONE clue, because this hiding was masterly: the bear can see the window from where it is.' Clues are honours paid to good hiding, never penalties for slow seeking.
  4. Hard-hiding stretch, for a room whose eyes have grown sharp: the bear hides with only ONE sign showing, the very tip of a nose at a cushion's edge, one paw's worth of shadow under the table, and the pack, told plainly that this hiding is the day's hardest, searches at its most ceremonious: two circuits, a clue, and the adult's looking-glass held in reserve. A finding at this level is celebrated at festival size, and the record says why while it happens: 'You found a NOSE. Rooms full of grown-ups walk past noses. This room hunts better than weather.' If the stretch's bear outlasts two clues, its hider reveals it standing beside the place like a lighthouse keeper, to applause: the bear was hidden by a master, and the record files the day accordingly.
  5. Finish with the bear's retirement: it is hidden one final time by the whole group together (eyes open, placing it in its favourite found-place, tucked with ceremony) and left there 'sleeping off the hunt' while the group files past it quietly. Then the tell-back, said at the door of the hunting ground: 'The bear hid five times. It was found five times, once by a nose. Nobody hid alone, nobody sought alone, and the finding belonged to everybody every time. That is how this room hunts: together, at walking pace, cheering.' On the next rainy day the bear will be found already hiding, because somebody, the adult, before breakfast, will have kept the game's promise overnight.

Variations

Easier: A two-hiding version: two places, two searches, the whole ceremony inside ten minutes for the newest hunting packs.

Harder: A torch version (a dim afternoon, a willing room): seekers carry one big torch between two hands, adult-led, and the bear is found by its lit ear in the gloom. The hunting ground's furniture pre-walked in the light, twice.

Safety

The laws are the safety: the bear hides, children do not. Nobody is ever hidden, enclosed, or left alone in this record, and the adult says so in the framing so the room's expectation is set before the first count. Hiding places live inside four fences: nothing that closes (no cupboards, boxes with lids, or doors that latch), nothing that tips or is climbed for, nothing with cords, and nothing at height. The bear is found by eyes at three-year-old altitude, standing on a floor that stays under every seeker. Eye-covering lasts one count of ten with bottoms on the floor; a child who will not cover is the count-keeper's partner at full honour, watching the adult's back instead. Seeking is walking, in the adult's company, as one pack: a search that scatters is re-gathered kindly at the bear's last known dignity, 'hunters together', because running searches in furnished rooms are how seekers meet chair legs. The bear itself is soft, washed, and seam-checked; a found bear is carried, never thrown back in triumph.

Inclusion notes

The hunt has a full set of honours and every one of them is the game: the hider chooses and places the bear from any position. Directing the adult's carrying hands by description ('behind the BIG cushion... the ear OUT') while the choice, the nerve, and the clue-giving stay the director's; the spotter's freeze-and-point can be a freeze-and-call, a freeze-and-stamp, or a held-up hand the pack has agreed means FOUND IT, and every form stops the hunt with the same authority. The clue-giver, keeper of the honours paid to good hiding, and the bear-carrier, who brings the rescued sailor home at full ceremony, rotate by list from any seat. A seeker who hunts better sitting still, watching the room's edges from the middle while the pack circuits, is posted as the room's lighthouse at full membership: spotting is spotting, and the lighthouse's bear is cheered like any other.
