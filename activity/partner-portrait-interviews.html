Partner Portrait Interviews

One deep interview per pair, notes kept, and then the introduction nobody else could give: you present the partner you actually met.

Ages
13–14
Group
4–30 kids
Time
30–45 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Write the three questions before you write anything else, and test each one against the framing's rule — could a stranger answer this honestly to somebody they met ten minutes ago, and would the room be glad it heard the answer? Cut any question that fishes for confession, ranking, money, family trouble or romance; a portrait interview earns depth by the quality of its attention, not by the sensitivity of its material. Keep the count at three and resist a fourth: depth in this format is manufactured by time per question, and a fourth question taxes the first three. Decide the session's shape now — draw, interview, write, introduce — with minutes per stage stated, because the introduction round will expand to fill any vagueness the earlier stages leave it, and the writing stage is always the one a loud session tries to steal from. Prepare the hat draw entire and check your pairs arithmetic for the room's awkward numbers: an odd count makes one trio, interviewed as a triangle, and the plan for it is made now, at the desk, not discovered apologetically at the draw.

Materials

  • the portrait questions, three only, agreed and written large before pairs are drawn (the session's whole constitution: questions that ask for a life, not a label — the place that made you, the skill you would teach, the thing you are quietly proud of — screened by the adult against the framing's one rule)
  • note cards and pencils, one card per interviewer (the portrait's evidence: notes taken in the partner's own words where they matter, because the introduction will quote them and a quotation must be earned)
  • a way of drawing pairs that nobody games (names from a hat, drawn by the adult in the open — the session's fairness is its first impression, and a rigged draw teaches the room the opposite of the session's lesson)
  • the portrait slate (the session's closing record: every pair, and beside each name the one line their partner's introduction was remembered by — kept by the session's keeper at the front)

Steps

  1. The framing, questions on the board, hat in the adult's hand: 'You introduce the person you actually met, not the one you expected. In the next half hour you will sit with one partner — drawn, not chosen, because chosen partners teach you about your friends and drawn partners teach you about people — and you will interview them under three questions only. Three questions is not a shortage; it is the discipline. You will have time to follow an answer where it goes — once, with one honest follow-up the questions allow: tell me more about that. Your card is for their words, not your summaries: when your partner says the exact sentence that is most them, it goes on the card in their words, inside invisible quotation marks you will treat as sacred when you stand up. Then you will introduce them to this room, and the room will judge your introduction by one standard only — did we meet the person you met? An introduction that performs the introducer has failed, however beautifully it is spoken. A plain one, with the true sentence in it, is the finest speaking this room does all year.' Pairs are drawn in the open, chairs are turned to face, and the room is given a full minute of settling before the first question is asked, because interviews begun in a scramble stay scrambled.
  2. The interview round, run in two halves so every partner is met both ways: the first interviewer asks, follows once where the answer invites it, and writes — and the session's writing law is stated before the round begins: notes are taken openly, on the card, where the partner can see them being taken, because a portrait sitting is a collaboration, not a surveillance; a partner may strike any line from the card by asking, at any moment, without giving a reason, and the line is struck in front of them. The adult calls the halfway turn by the clock the framing promised — interviews are not ended by energy but by agreement — and roles reverse entire: second interviewer, same three questions, same follow-up law, same card discipline. Pairs who finish early do not start a second, shallower interview; they sit with their cards and find the sentence, because the introduction they are about to give is made of the finding, and a pair still talking when the writing stage begins will give the room the talk instead of the portrait.
  3. The writing stage, ten honest minutes of quiet: each interviewer builds the introduction from the card alone, in the session's taught shape — the name, said first and said right (checked with the partner in a whisper before the round: pronunciation is respect, rehearsed, never improvised in public); the place that made them, in a line; the skill they would teach, in a line; the quiet pride, in their own quoted sentence where the card holds one; and the introducer's one closing line, the only place the introducer's own voice is licensed: what meeting this person added to the room. Introductions are written to be spoken inside a minute — the keeper holds the session's gentle clock, announced now so brevity is a shared discipline rather than a public correction — and a writer stuck for the closing line is given the session's loan of one: begin with the words 'we are luckier because', and let the card finish the sentence.
  4. The introduction round, the session's whole point, run as a ceremony of attention: pairs come forward together; the introducer speaks; the introduced stands beside them and is looked at by the room as a person met, not a specimen shown. The introduced partner holds one right, stated in the framing and honoured without debate: at the introduction's end they may add one sentence of correction or completion — 'all true, and also this' — which the keeper adds to the slate in their words, because a portrait's subject is its final editor. Applause is for the pair, both halves of it, and the keeper chalks the slate's line for each introduced name from the introduction's own best sentence, chosen by the room's murmur rather than the keeper's taste. Nobody is introduced by anyone they were not paired with, nobody's portrait is given in their absence, and a member who must leave before the round has their introduction given first, by agreement, or held unspoken — the session would rather keep one portrait back than give it to an empty chair.
  5. Closing, the slate read back and the cards given away: the keeper reads the slate's lines entire, name by name, the way a register is read — the room hearing itself as a gallery of met people — and then the cards change hands by the session's last law: your card about your partner is given to your partner, to keep, correct, or throw away as theirs, because the evidence of a portrait belongs to its subject once the introduction is spoken. The tell-back is said with the empty hat on the table: 'An hour ago this room was names and expectations. It is a gallery now: every person here was met by one pair of ears that was not free to wander, quoted in their own words, struck of anything they struck, and introduced by the only standard that matters — the person we met is the person you described. Drawn partners, three questions, one true sentence each. That is all a portrait has ever needed.' The slate's lines are copied into the session's record by the keeper, and the hat is stored with the question cards for the next room that needs meeting properly.

Variations

Easier: A two-question version (a short session or a young-feeling room): the place and the skill, portraits at the same discipline in less time — the pride question is added at the session's second running, when the room has learned the laws.

Harder: A return version (a group that has sat once): the same draw is kept, the questions deepen by agreement — the lesson learned the hard way, the person who taught you — and introductions are given from the new cards alone, with the first session's slate read at the close as the room's earlier gallery.

⚠️ Safety

This session's safety is the safety of being known, and its laws are stated in the framing because they are the framing: the questions are screened by the adult before the room sees them — nothing that fishes for confession, family trouble, money, romance, belief or difference — and any question that drifts there in a follow-up is the adult's to redirect, kindly and at once, because follow-ups are where screened questions go to become unscreened ones. The strike law is absolute and is the session's load-bearing rule: a partner may strike any line, any answer, any topic, at any moment, without a reason, and the striking is done visibly and without comment, by interviewer and room alike; an introduction that smuggles a struck line back in as a joke has broken the session's constitution and the adult ends that introduction there. Notes are open, cards are surrendered to their subjects at the close, and nothing from any interview is repeated outside the room by the session's members as gossip — what a portrait sitting hears is the room's, in the room, for the introduction. A member who would rather not sit is never argued with: the session always holds one full office — keeper of the slate — that participates entire without being interviewed or introduced, and taking it needs no explanation then or ever.

🤝 Inclusion notes

The portrait sitting is built so that attention, not performance, is its currency, and its offices reflect that: the keeper of the slate — the session's memory, its clock, its register-reader — is a full office held from the front, at the session's ceremonial centre. Interviews can be conducted in whatever register a pair shares: spoken, written question-and-answer on the cards themselves, signed, or through the session's ordinary patience with a partner's own pace and pauses — the three questions are the same, the follow-up law is the same, and a written interview's card is evidence of exactly the same weight. Note-taking welcomes every hand: notes may be written, dictated back to the partner for correction as they are made — some sitters prefer this, and the session treats it as a style, not a concession — or kept as the session's symbols and tallies where a card's keeper has agreed their key with their partner. Introductions may be read from the card, spoken from memory, or read by the keeper at the introducer's request, at identical honour: the standard is whether the room met the person, never how the meeting was voiced. Pairs are drawn by the hat for everyone alike, seating is turned chair-to-chair wherever the pair sits best, and the introduced partner's right of correction — the session's final editorial word — belongs to every subject without exception or assistance unless they ask for it.
