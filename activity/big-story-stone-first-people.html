Big Story Stone, First People

One smooth stone passed hand to hand in the story circle: today the grown-up tells, plainly and truly, whose land this classroom stands on.

Before you begin

Ages
3–3
Group
4–14 kids
Time
10–15 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
No prep needed

Setup

Choose and wash the stone ahead, and, this is the real preparation, learn whose land your classroom actually stands on before the circle sits down: the name of the Indigenous people of your place, said correctly, and two or three true, plain things about them that a three-year-old can hold (they lived here first; many are still here, our neighbours now; they cared for this land a very long time). If you do not know, find out from your library or your district's own land statement before this record runs. The record refuses to be run on a guess, because a story told wrong at three gets remembered wrong at thirty. Practise saying the people's name aloud until it is ordinary in your mouth.

Materials

  • one big smooth story stone, adult-chosen (palm-sized or bigger, washed, no sharp edges, no cracks. The circle's talking piece, held by whoever is telling or listening hardest)
  • no other materials. The story is carried in words

Steps

  1. Gather the circle, hold the stone in both hands, and say the plain framing in full: 'This land had people on it before there were towns, before there were schools, before there was us. The first people here were the [name, said plainly and correctly]. They are still here. They are our neighbours. Today is a day for saying their name properly and hearing one true story about them.' State the one rule: 'The stone is held in two hands, in a lap. When the stone is with you, you may hold it quietly or add one true-sized thing. The stone is never thrown, tapped, or passed over anybody's head.'
  2. First telling together, yours: with the stone in your lap, tell the short true story you prepared, three or four sentences, no more: who the people are, one thing they made or did on this land ('they fished this river'), one thing that is still true now ('their children go to school in our town'). Keep it at story size, not lesson size: names, a river, a neighbour. When your telling is done, say the closing line of every turn, which the circle will learn to say with you: 'That is a true story, and it is still true.'
  3. Stone round: the stone travels hand to hand round the circle in order, low, two hands to two hands. Each holder may add ONE small true thing, something from the story retold in their own words, something true about this place ('the river is near our park'), or simply the people's name said carefully, which counts in full and is honoured at full size: a name said right by a three-year-old is this record's whole curriculum arriving. A holder who only wants to hold the stone and pass it on has also taken a complete turn.
  4. Listening stretch, offered once the stone has travelled: the adult wonders aloud, gently, 'The first people knew this land before anybody built on it. What do YOU know about our place that somebody new wouldn't?', and the circle offers its own land knowledge at three-year-old scale: where the puddles gather, which tree drops the best sticks, where the sun sits at snack time. The point is made without being announced: knowing a place by living in it is a real kind of knowing, and it is the kind the first people had most of all.
  5. Finish with the stone retired to its place, a shelf or cloth where it lives between tellings, carried there low in two hands by the last holder, and the tell-back said over the empty circle: 'We said the [name] people's name properly. We heard a true story about the first people of this place, and every part of it is still true. The stone will remember it for next time.'

Variations

Easier: A name-and-one-thing version: the framing, your telling, and the stone travelling once with only names and quiet holds, the whole ceremony inside ten minutes.

Harder: A place-walk coda (dry day only): after the circle, the group walks to one real place the story named, the river's fence, the old tree, and the stone, carried by the adult, 'visits' it; the circle says the closing line there, outdoors, once.

Safety

The stone is palm-sized or bigger, smooth, washed, and checked before every session; anything cracked or sharp-edged leaves the set permanently. It travels low, lap to lap, in two hands, and is never tossed or handed over another child's head. The adult's hands shadow the pass for the youngest holders. The content rule is the record's other safety: only true, plain things are told, no invented ceremonies, no costumes, no 'playing' at being the people being honoured, ever; this record teaches about real neighbours with respect, and the adult corrects a drift toward pretend instantly and kindly.

Source: not listed yet.

Inclusion notes

Holding the stone asks nothing of a voice: a holder can add their true thing in words, in a sign, in one word plus a point out the window toward the place itself, or by tapping the stone twice to ask the circle to say the people's name together for them. Every form closes with the same line, said by all. The stone can travel on a lap tray between neighbours for any holder whose hands are busy elsewhere; the turn, the timing, and the honour are identical. The closing-line caller, the voice that leads 'That is a true story, and it is still true', is the circle's official honour and rotates by list.
