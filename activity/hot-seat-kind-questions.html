Hot Seat, Kind Questions

One chair, the whole group's kind attention, and questions screened in advance. The seat is warm, never hot, and the pass is absolute.

Before you begin

Ages
13–14
Group
6–30 kids
Time
20–30 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Screen the cards like the session depends on it, because it does: read every submitted question yourself and apply the record's constitution, which you will state in the framing. Questions ask about tastes, memories, skills, opinions, hopes, and harmless hypotheticals; no question touches bodies or appearance, money, family circumstances, romance, health, grades, or anybody else's business, and no question is a dare, a ranking of people in the room, or a story its answerer has not volunteered to tell. Cards that fail are removed quietly, without naming their writers. The screening is a filter, not a court. Shuffle the survivors, count them against the session's length (a seat runs about four questions; programme fewer seats than the group wants, so the record ends with appetite, a discipline every good host learns), and arrange the chair and circle so the seated person faces friends, not a firing line: a shallow arc, the adult seated inside the circle as a member with a job, never opposite the seat like an interviewer.

Materials

  • the question cards (written by the group in a prior five minutes and screened by the adult before the session. Every card a question its writer would happily answer themselves, in front of everyone, which is the screening's whole test)
  • one chair, placed where the whole circle can see it kindly (the seat, ordinary, comfortable, and deliberately not special-looking; the attention is the event, not the furniture)
  • a soft pass token held by whoever sits in the seat (a smooth stone or a beanbag. Passing is done by raising it, so a pass never has to be spoken, explained, or performed)

Steps

  1. The framing, the seat empty, the group in its arc: 'This is the hot seat, and in this room the name is a lie we are correcting together: the seat is warm, never hot. One person sits. The group asks. The questions are on these cards, every one of them screened, every one of them a question its writer would answer themselves. And these are the seat's laws, which outrank everybody's curiosity, including mine: the pass is a complete answer. Raise the token, the card is gone, no reason given, no reason asked, ever. An answer can be as long or as short as its owner makes it. One word is a whole answer. Nobody repeats what is said in the seat outside this room: we are practising the difference between a group that knows things about each other and a group that trades in them, and only the first kind is being built here.' The first seat is taken by the adult or a confident volunteer, at full ceremony, so the laws are seen working, including a pass, used early and used proudly, before anyone warier is asked to sit.
  2. The asking round: cards are drawn by the questioner, read aloud exactly as written, and answered from the seat. The group's craft is listening at full size, no follow-up interrogation, no cross-examination disguised as interest; one follow-up is allowed, and its form is fixed: 'say more about that, if you'd like', an invitation whose refusal costs nothing and is taken at face value the instant it is given. Laughter is welcome where the answer invites it and is checked where it doesn't, and the adult's ear is the session's thermostat: the moment a laugh lands ON somebody rather than WITH them, the next card is drawn and the moment is over, kindly and finally. Answers that surprise the group, the quiet person's enormous opinion, the joker's serious hope, are received at the size they are given, because receiving is the skill the seat exists to teach.
  3. Rotation: seats change by volunteer and by gentle invitation, never by assignment or acclamation. Nobody is put in the seat by the group's enthusiasm, which is pressure wearing a friendly coat. A person who declines the seat holds full membership of the session: they draw cards, they ask, they listen, and the framing protects the symmetry ('asking well is half this record; some of its best practitioners never sit'). Each seat ends the same way: the group names, briefly and specifically, one thing it now knows that it is glad to know, 'I did not know you could juggle; I am glad I know', and the seated person steps back into the arc to that sentence, which is the seat's whole social architecture: you were attended to, you were received kindly, and now you are one of us who knows it can be survived, even enjoyed.
  4. The question-craft stretch, once the rotation has warmed: the group reviews the cards that landed best, not the funniest answers, the best QUESTIONS: the ones whose answers nobody could have predicted and everybody wanted to hear, and the session names what made them good, in the group's own words, recorded by the adult on the back of the running order: openness (a hundred honest answers fit through it), safety (its answerer could not lose by answering), and surprise (it asked about a country nobody knew the person had visited in their own life). Each member then writes one new card to that standard, screened on the spot, and the session's final seat is taken, by a volunteer the new cards have tempted, using only the new deck: the group asks its own best questions last, and feels the difference in the answers.
  5. Closing: the cards are gathered, the pass token is retired to its place, and the tell-back is said with the seat empty at the circle's centre: 'We know more about each other than we did, and none of it cost anybody anything. Every pass was honoured, every answer was sized by its owner, and what was said here stays here. That is not a small thing. Most rooms a person will ever sit in do not run on those laws. This one did, because you kept them.' The screened deck is kept as the group's own deck, added to over the year, its constitution unchanged: a deck of questions a group wrote for itself, to its own standard, is a portrait of what that group finds interesting about being people. Worth keeping as carefully as the answers were heard.

Variations

Easier: A three-question version: one seat, three cards, the laws at full strength inside ten minutes. A standing opener a group can run whenever a new member joins, with the new member asking first and sitting only if they offer.

Harder: A written version (a group that prefers paper): seats are taken in writing. A member's name drawn, the group writes kind questions anonymously to the screened standard, the seated member chooses three to answer aloud and passes the rest unread by anyone else, ever.

Safety

The constitution is the safety system and it is enforced by structure, not by vigilance alone: cards are screened by the adult before the session and the off-limits territories are named in the framing, bodies and appearance, money, family circumstances, romance, health, grades, third parties' business, so no answerer is ever ambushed by a question the room had already agreed not to ask. The pass is absolute, token-raised, unexplained, and unexamined, and the adult models it in the first seat so its honour is established before it is needed. Nothing is recorded, photographed, or repeated outside the room: the session's confidentiality law is stated in the framing and the adult keeps it visibly, including afterwards. An answer quoted back in a corridor, however affectionately, ends the record's future with that group until trust is rebuilt, and the framing says so plainly because teenagers keep laws they understand the reason for. The adult sits in the circle and watches the laughter's direction the whole session; a seat is ended early, without ceremony or blame, the moment its warmth changes character.

Inclusion notes

The seat is taken in whatever way its sitter communicates best: answers can be spoken, signed, written on the spot and read by a chosen voice, or given as the briefest signal the laws protect. One word is a whole answer here, by constitution. Asking is an equal discipline: a questioner can read their card, hand it to the adult to read verbatim, or point to the card drawn for them, and the question loses nothing by its route. The pass token works for every sitter identically, raised, not spoken, which is precisely why the pass was designed as an object: nobody in this record has to find words in order to decline. The arc is arranged so the seat faces the group at whatever height and distance its next sitter chooses, the chair is an ordinary chair among chairs, and membership of the session, asking, listening, naming the glad-to-know sentence at each seat's end, is complete from any position in the arc, including the position of never sitting at all.
