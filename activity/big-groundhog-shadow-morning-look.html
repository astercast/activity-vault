Big Groundhog Shadow Morning Look

February the second: a soft groundhog peeks out of its burrow box, and the whole group checks the sunny patch. Is anybody's shadow out today?

Before you begin

Ages
3–3
Group
3–14 kids
Time
10–15 min
Where
Indoors & Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Cut the burrow door yourself and check the box the way this record checks everything: by trying it. The box must stand steady when the groundhog is pushed through its door a dozen times, and its edges must be tape-smooth, a burrow that collapses on its tenant teaches slapstick, not February. That morning, not the day before, stand in the shadow-checking place yourself and learn what kind of morning it is: if the sun is out, your own shadow will be standing there to meet you, and the record will run on the real thing; if the sky is grey wall to wall, set the torch across the floorboards instead and plan to name it honestly in the framing ('the sun is behind the clouds today, so our lamp will hold the sun's job'). Knowing which morning you have before the circle gathers is the whole preparation.

Materials

  • one soft toy groundhog (or a brown sock puppet with two ears sewn or tied on. A groundhog the room can greet by name; soft, washed, and big enough to see across a circle)
  • one big cardboard burrow box (a box with a round door cut by the adult, big enough for the groundhog to peek from and for nobody to climb into, its door faces the group)
  • one sunny patch of ground or floor by a window (the shadow-checking place, found by the adult that same morning; on a grey morning, a bright torch shone across the floor is the honest stand-in and is named as one)

Steps

  1. Gather the group where every eye can see the burrow box and say the plain framing: 'Today is the second of February, and people tell a story about this day. That a groundhog comes out of its burrow, and if it sees its shadow, winter stays a while longer. It is a story people love. It is not a weather report. A groundhog cannot call the spring, and neither can we. But the story is ABOUT something real, and we can check the real thing ourselves: shadows. When the sun is out, shadows come out. When the clouds cover the sun, they do not. Today we check.' State the laws whole: 'The groundhog lives in the adult's hands or in its burrow. It is greeted, never grabbed. Shadow-checking is standing still and looking DOWN: eyes look at the ground, never up at the sun, ever. Reports are given in the day's two words, and both words are good news.'
  2. First peeking together: the burrow door faces the group, the adult's hand goes in slow, and the groundhog comes out the way the story says, nose first, then ears, then the whole round body, blinking at the room. The group greets it by name, and then the record's real question is put to it at full ceremony: 'Groundhog. The story says you look for your shadow. We will help you look, because looking is a job this room is good at.' The groundhog is carried to the shadow-checking place by the adult, held low where a groundhog lives, and the group watches the ground beneath it like a jury: is there a groundhog-shaped shadow on the ground, or is there not?
  3. Shadow-checking round: whatever the groundhog's report, the children check their own, one at a time, standing in the patch at full stillness, feet planted, arms a little out, eyes DOWN at the evidence, while the room calls the finding in the session's official shape: 'SHADOW OUT' (a dark self standing on the ground, waving when its owner waves, which the owner is invited to test exactly once) or 'NO SHADOW' (the ground bare of any second self, the light flat, the clouds holding the whole sky). Both reports are received at identical size, and the record says why while it happens: 'A no-shadow morning is not a broken morning. It is a cloudy one. The groundhog's story works on cloudy mornings too. That is most of why people kept telling it.'
  4. Story stretch, once every checker has a report: the adult tells the last piece plainly, because this record does not let a story borrow science's coat: 'In the story, a shadow means six more weeks of winter. In the real world, the one we just checked with our own eyes, a shadow means the sun is out RIGHT NOW, on this patch of ground. It says nothing about next week. Next week will bring its own mornings, and we will check those too.' Then the group's own morning is named and filed in the day's official sentence, said together: 'On OUR February the second, the report was: SHADOW OUT' (or NO SHADOW) ', seen with our own eyes, on our own ground.'
  5. Finish with the groundhog returned to its burrow at full ceremony, backwards in, the way groundhogs in stories go, to sleep until the story needs it again, and the tell-back said over the closed door: 'The story said a groundhog would look. We looked WITH it, and then we looked for ourselves, every one of us, eyes down, standing still. Whatever the morning was, we know what it was, because we checked. That is worth more than the story, and the story is worth quite a lot.' The burrow box lives on the shelf until spring, and on any sunny morning before then, any child may point at a shadow and file the report again: it stays official all season.

Variations

Easier: A two-checker version: the groundhog and one volunteer check, the group reports, the whole ceremony inside ten minutes for the newest circles.

Harder: A torch version (a grey week, no sun at all): the checking place is a dark corner and a big torch, every child checks by lamplight across several mornings, and the day the real sun finally does the job is celebrated as the record's true February the second, whatever the calendar says.

Safety

Eyes look down, always: nobody looks at the sun in this record, and the adult says it in the framing and watches for it in the round, a child who tips their face up is brought back to the evidence kindly ('the shadow is down HERE, with us'). The checking patch is walked by the adult that morning for ice, puddles hiding ice, and anything the ground is keeping; standing still is the game's whole motion, and it is done on ground the adult would stand on in socks. The burrow box is adult-cut, tape-smoothed, and furniture, not a toy: nobody climbs in, on, or through it, and a box that softens or tips is retired to the recycling with the groundhog's thanks. The torch stand-in is an adult-held lamp, never passed hand to hand, and its light is shone across the floor, at the ground, like the sun's job requires, never into anybody's eyes.

Source: Wikipedia: Groundhog Day

Inclusion notes

Checking works from any position the patch allows: a seated checker checks from a chair set in the sunny place, a shadow keeps no opinions about the height it is cast from, and a checker's wave can be a raised hand, a turned head, or a held-up scarf, each answered by the shadow identically and reported at the same size. The reports are the record's honours and they come in every register the room owns: spoken, signed, stamped on the floor with one foot for SHADOW OUT, or given by the checker pointing at the group's two big report cards, which the adult holds up one at a time. The groundhog's carrier, walking beside the adult as the tenant travels to the checking place, hands ready under it, is the morning's official second honour and rotates by list.
