Compost Heap Temperature Ledger

A working compost heap run by teens: built in layers, turned on a rota, and read weekly by thermometer — the heat is the evidence it is alive.

Ages
13–14
Group
4–16 kids
Time
45–60 min
Where
Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Walk the site before the group is offered the heap, and choose it like the season-long commitment it is: the bay must drain after rain rather than drown, sit where a loaded fork can be carried without crossing play space, and stand on bare earth if the land allows, because a heap is in conversation with the ground under it. Stock the two bins before building day — a heap cannot be layered from good intentions — and test the thermometer in warm water against a second reading so the crew's first core temperature is taken on an instrument that has already told the truth once. Read the scraps rule to yourself until you can say it without notes: vegetable matter only, nothing that was ever meat, dairy, oil or cooked food, because the rule is what keeps a heap a heap instead of a nuisance. Set the ledger's first pages up in advance — build date, layers as planned, the turning rota's empty grid, and the temperature table with its date, weather, and reading columns — so the book is ready to be the record from the first forkful.

Materials

  • a compost bay or a marked heap site in a corner the adults approve (shaded, drained, reachable with a full fork in all weathers, and far enough from eating areas that the heap's working smell is the heap's own business)
  • a long-stem compost thermometer (the heap's instrument — pushed to the core, left to settle, read in place; no other thermometer in the building reaches the middle of a heap and survives)
  • greens and browns stock, kept separately until layering (grass clippings, kitchen vegetable scraps with no meat, no dairy and no oils among them; and dry leaves, shredded card, or straw — the browns bin should never be the empty one)
  • a garden fork per turning pair, work gloves for every hand that touches the heap, and the ledger (a hardback book that lives with the adults — the heap's memory across the whole season)

Steps

  1. Building day, the framing said at the empty bay: 'The heap is alive, and the ledger is how you know. A compost heap is not a rubbish pile with good PR — it is a city of small lives eating what we layer, and like any city it has a temperature, a diet, and weather. We build it in layers today: browns, greens, browns, greens, each layer damp as a wrung cloth, nothing packed down hard, air left in the structure because the citizens breathe. Then we keep it. A rota turns it, a thermometer reads it every week on the same day, and the ledger records what the heap actually did — rose, fell, stalled — because a heap you only visit when it smells interesting is a story, and a heap with a ledger is a season of evidence.' The layers go in as described, each one named aloud as it lands, the first temperature taken at the core before the group leaves, however unimpressive the number — day one's reading is the baseline every later reading is honest against.
  2. The weekly round, run by the rota pair whose names head that week's page: the thermometer goes to the core at three points, settles, and is read in place, and all three readings go in the book with the day's weather beside them, because a heap read without its weather is a number without a season. The pair inspects as they read: moisture squeezed from a gloved handful (a drop or two is right; a stream means too wet and the remedy is browns and a turn; dust means too dry and the remedy is water and a turn), and the heap's smell reported in the ledger's plain categories — earthy, sweet, sharp, wrong — with sharp or wrong triggering a turning there and then rather than a note for next week. Readings are never rounded toward the interesting: a falling temperature is written as falling, in the same ink as a rise, because the ledger's authority is exactly that it does not care which way the news points.
  3. Turning day, when the rota calls it or the readings demand it: the heap is forked from one bay to the next (or onto its sheet and back), outside to the middle, middle to the outside, so every part of the city gets its season in the hot core and its season at the cool edge. Turners work in pairs with gloves on, forks carried point-down between lifts, and anything that should not be there — the plastic, the stray can, the scraps-rule violation somebody's lunch left behind — is lifted out and recorded in the ledger without drama: contamination found is a finding, and the book keeps findings. A turn is also the heap's census: the pair notes what has vanished (layered greens rarely survive a fortnight recognisable), what persists (woody browns keep their shape longest, which is information about carbon, not failure), and what has arrived — the worms, the beetles, the white threads of the heap's working citizens — each written as observed, never as decoration.
  4. The stall protocol, taught before the first stall so nobody improvises: when the ledger shows two flat or falling readings in a row, the crew reads the book backwards before touching the heap — moisture notes, the last turn's date, the layers since — and names the likeliest cause in the ledger from the evidence the book itself holds: too dry, too wet, too many greens, too few, cold weather slowing the whole city honestly. One remedy is applied, one only, and written beside the prediction it carries ('added two barrows of browns and turned — expect a rise within the week'), so the next reading can grade the reasoning and not just the heap. A stall that survives two honest remedies is reported to the adults as a stall, with the book open at the evidence — the record's rule is that a heap is allowed to refuse, and a crew that reports a refusal with its workings has done the job properly.
  5. Season's end, the harvest of the heap: the finished material is sieved by the crew — the fine, dark, earthy fraction goes to the beds in barrows, credited in the ledger with its weight or barrow count and the beds it fed — and the coarse fraction goes back as the starter layer of the next heap, carrying the city's citizens with it. The closing page is written together at the bay: the season's highest core reading and its date, the fastest rise after a turn, the stall that taught the most, and the one instruction the crew would hand the next crew, earned at fork length. The tell-back is said over the closed book: 'This heap ate a season of what the site threw away and paid it back as soil, and we know that the way you know anything — because we measured it, weekly, in the rain, and wrote down the readings we did not like in the same ink as the ones we did. The heap was alive. The ledger is how we know. Next season's crew starts from your book, not from zero.' The thermometer is washed, dried, and stored with the ledger where the next crew will find both together.

Variations

Easier: A two-bay version (sites with a second bay): two heaps run a fortnight apart on the same rota, and the ledger keeps a comparison page — two cities, one weather, and the differences between their curves are where the season's real reading lives.

Harder: A winter-watch version: the heap is built in autumn and only read, never turned, through the cold months — the ledger tracks how long the core holds its heat against the season, and the spring turn is graded against the winter's curve.

⚠️ Safety

Gloves are the heap's uniform: no bare hand goes into the heap, ever, because a working heap holds sharp surprises — glass, thorns, the fork somebody lost — alongside its citizens, and its warmth is not a cleanliness certificate. Forks travel point-down, rest point-down, and are counted out and back like the tools they are; turning pairs keep a fork's length from each other and nobody forks toward another person. The scraps rule is absolute and hygienic as much as horticultural: no meat, no dairy, no oils, no cooked food — a heap fed wrongly draws the wrong visitors and earns the wrong smells, and a violation found is removed the day it is found. Hands are washed with soap after every heap session, before food, without exception or shortcut, and the thermometer is washed after every reading round for the same reason. Anyone with a cut on a working hand covers it before gloves go on; the heap will still be there next week, and the rota has understudies by design.

🤝 Inclusion notes

Heap work divides into jobs at every strength: the thermometer is read by one pair of hands and called aloud to the ledger-keeper, who may be seated at the bay's edge in the dry with the book — the readings, the weather column and the stall reasoning are the heap's intellectual core, and they are a seated job done at full authority. Turning itself scales honestly: a fork can be worked by a pair sharing the lift, the sieve can be shaken by turns or rocked by two, and the barrow runs to the beds go at the carrier's pace over the route the crew has walked for firm, step-free ground before building day. The ledger welcomes every register — a turning note can be written, dictated to the keeper verbatim, or given as the round's spoken report the keeper records with the reporter's name attached. The rota is published a season ahead, so every crew member's weeks are known, swappable by agreement, and never sprung on anyone — a heap kept by rota is a heap nobody has to be surprised by.
