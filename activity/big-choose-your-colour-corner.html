Big Choose Your Colour Corner

Four big colours, one in each corner. Walk to the colour you like best today, stand with your colour, and wave to the other corners. Tomorrow may differ.

Before you begin

Ages
3–3
Group
4–16 kids
Time
10–15 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
No prep needed

Setup

Lay the four corners before the group gathers and then walk them yourself, slowly, at three-year-old eye height: each colour must read clearly across the room (a yellow card on a pale floor in bright window light can wash into a rumour, move it into shade or back it with something dark), each corner must have standing room for more children than you expect to choose it, because a colour everybody loves is a crowd, and a crowd in a corner needs its ground planned, not discovered, and the walking lanes between the middle and every corner must be clear of the furniture that migrates into rooms like this. Learn the record's one kindness in advance: you will not balance the corners. If eleven children choose blue, blue holds eleven, and the corner's size is reported as joy, not corrected as a problem.

Materials

  • four big colour mats, one per corner of the room (red, blue, yellow, green, mats or big cards laid flat and weighted or taped at their corners so no edge can curl under a small foot; the colours are the whole equipment)

Steps

  1. Gather the group in the room's middle and say the plain framing: 'Look at the corners. This room is wearing four colours today, red there, blue there, yellow there, green there. In a moment, each of you will walk to ONE colour: the one you like best TODAY. Not forever. Not for a team. Just the colour your eyes are happiest standing in this morning. You will stand with your colour, and your colour's people will stand with you, and we will see what this room likes today.' State the laws whole: 'Choosing is walking. Nobody runs to a colour; a colour is worth walking to. Colours are not teams: nobody recruits, nobody persuades, nobody says a colour is wrong. A colour cannot be wrong; it can only be somebody's favourite, or not yours. Changing your mind is allowed, in the second round, anybody may walk to a new colour, and the room will be glad to watch them go.'
  2. First choosing together, yours: name your own colour at full ceremony and walk to it at the record's official pace, talking your choosing out loud the way the room will need to hear choosing done: 'My eyes keep going to the yellow. Yellow is standing in the sun over there and it looks like morning. I am choosing yellow, today. Yesterday I might have chosen blue. Tomorrow is tomorrow's business.' Stand in your corner and be counted there, visibly, an adult alone in a colour with nobody yet beside you, because the room must see that a corner of one is a complete corner before anyone is asked to risk being one.
  3. Choosing round: the group chooses at the adult's word, walking, in all directions at once, the room briefly a slow weather of small people crossing to their colours, and settles. Then the corners are greeted in the session's official ceremony: each corner, in turn, WAVES to the other three and calls its colour ('BLUE corner. Here we are!'), and the other corners wave back, because the record's whole sociology is in that wave: different colours, one room, everybody visible to everybody. The counts are taken by the corner-callers and given in the official shape. 'Blue holds five. Red holds two. Yellow holds three. Green holds ONE, and one is a whole corner', the smallest corner named at exactly the same size as the largest, by design, every time.
  4. Second choosing stretch, the record's heart: after a minute of standing in colour, the adult opens the floor's one great permission: 'Second choosing. Your feet may keep your colour, or they may take you somewhere new, if your eyes have changed their minds while standing here. Changing is not cheating. Changing is looking twice.' Whoever walks, walks at the same unhurried pace, received by their new corner with the wave, and whoever stays is named kindly too ('Some colours hold their people. Staying is also a choice, and the room can see it'). The new counts are taken and the CHANGES are reported as the day's real news, ahead of the numbers: 'Two people changed. One went from red to green because, ' (the changer, if they wish, gives their reason in any size: 'Green looked quieter' is a complete philosophy) ', and the room is glad to know it.'
  5. Finish with the whole group walking back to the middle together, colours dissolving kindly into the room again, and the tell-back said in the middle where it started: 'This room, today: blue five, yellow three, red two, green one, and two changers, whose eyes looked twice. Nobody was wrong, nobody was recruited, and every corner was waved at by every other. When somebody new joins this room, the colours will be the first thing we show them: choose one, stand in it, be waved at. It is the quickest way we know to be a person in a new room.' The mats stay down through the day as the room's quiet geography. Children will drift to their colours at odd moments, and the record lets them: a corner chosen in the morning is a place to stand in all day.

Variations

Easier: A two-colour version: red and blue only, two corners, the whole record inside ten minutes for the very newest groups. Choosing between two is already choosing.

Harder: A family version: the colours are chosen for somebody else first, 'walk to the colour your grandmother would like', and the room hears the reasons, which arrive as small stories about people who are not in the room.

Safety

Choosing is walking, enforced kindly from the first crossing: four directions of travel at once is a small traffic system, and its only rule that matters is pace. A child whose excitement runs is walked back to the middle by the adult's company and sent again at walking pace, without loss of colour or dignity. The mats lie dead flat, corners weighted or taped, edges checked by the adult's own sliding foot before the group gathers: a mat that can skate is a card on the floor or nothing. Corners are furniture-cleared with standing room and a clear wall behind them. No corner sits beside a door that opens into it, a shelf that can be pulled, or the room's traffic lane to the toilet. Nobody is ever left standing alone in a corner as a spectacle: the adult's first choosing plants an adult visibly content in a corner of one, and if the round still produces a corner of one child, the corner-caller's report names it 'a whole corner' before any other size is mentioned.

Inclusion notes

Choosing is done with the feet when the feet are willing, and at the same size by every other road: a chooser can be carried to their colour by the room's ordinary way of travelling, point at the colour card the adult holds up and be walked the choice by a partner's company, or hold their colour's small card in the middle while the corner waves to THEM, the wave travels both directions in this record, and a corner's people can be counted around a seated chooser exactly as around a standing one. Reasons arrive in any register, a word, a point at something in the room wearing the colour ('blue, like THAT cup'), a held-up card, and changers are honoured identically however the change is announced, including by simply arriving. The corner-callers, who give the room's counts in the official shape, rotate by list and may call from inside their own colour, which is where the authority comes from anyway.
