Teach-Back to the Youngers

Teens prepare and deliver a real ten-minute session for younger children: rehearsed, supervised, and marked only by the youngers' attention.

Ages 13–14 · 40–60 min · 4 materials

Ages
13–14
Group
4–20 kids
Time
40–60 min
Where
Indoors & Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Arrange the session like the responsibility it is: agree the youngers' group, their adult's constant presence, the room, and the time with everyone concerned before any planning begins. The teach-back never happens by ambush, and the youngers' adult is a partner in it, briefed on the plan and holding their ordinary authority throughout. Set the planning bar in advance and in writing: ONE skill, small enough to be true ('by the end, the youngers will be able to tie a reef knot / fold a paper crane that flies / keep a four-count rhythm and stop together'), teachable in ten minutes to the age they actually are, and require each pair to state the youngers' age and plan to that age's real attention, real hands, real vocabulary, which the pairs research by asking the youngers' adult three questions beforehand (what do they love, what do they find hard, what words won't land). Prepare the room for two audiences at once: the youngers' working space at their height, the teachers' standing places, and a clock the teachers can see, because the ten minutes is a promise.

Materials

  • the teach-back plans (one per teaching pair, on one page: what the youngers will be able to DO at the end that they could not do at the start, one skill, named in one sentence, the steps to it, and the plan's honest timing, rehearsed against a clock)
  • materials for the skill, in the youngers' sizes and numbers (prepared by the teaching pair, counted, with spares. A session that runs out of materials teaches only that its teachers ran out)
  • the youngers themselves, a small group by arrangement with their own adult (six to ten children, whose adult stays in the room throughout. The session's safeguarding shape, stated to everyone in advance)
  • a feedback card for the youngers' adult (three prompts only: where the youngers' attention was fullest, where it thinned, and one thing the teachers did that worked, observed, specific, and kind)

Steps

  1. The framing, to the teaching pairs before the youngers arrive: 'Teaching is the final exam nobody grades. You have planned a session for real children, who will not pretend to learn to be polite. Their attention is the honest mark scheme: when it is full, your teaching is working; when it thins, that is information about the teaching, in the moment, and good teachers read it and adjust. They do not blame the class. The laws are these, and they are the profession's own: the youngers' adult is in charge of the youngers, always. Discipline is never your job, behaviour is never your problem to solve in front of the room, and any child who needs something beyond your session goes to their adult with your help, kindly, at once. You never touch the youngers except a offered handshake or high-five they initiate. You teach in pairs so one of you can always be watching the room while the other demonstrates. And you will open by learning their names, because a teacher who does not know the class's names is performing at them.'
  2. Rehearsal, with the clock: pairs run their sessions to each other, full size, at the youngers' imagined height and pace, demonstrations done slowly, instructions given in the short sentences the plan promised, and the watching pairs marking against the plan's one question: could a child DO the next step from what was just said and shown, without guessing? Every place a watching pair has to guess is a place the plan is rewritten, in the room, before the youngers come near it. Timing is rehearsed honestly: a session that runs fourteen minutes in rehearsal is a ten-minute session with four minutes of somebody's material still in it, and the cutting is done at rehearsal, kindly and by its owners. A tighter session that lands beats a complete one that dissolves, and the youngers' thinning attention at minute eleven will deliver the same verdict less kindly if the pairs do not deliver it first.
  3. The session itself: the youngers arrive with their adult, are greeted by name as the pairs learn them, and the teach-back opens with the teachers' one-sentence promise, said to the children directly: 'By the end, you will be able to, ' and the skill, named plainly, so the youngers know what the ten minutes is FOR. Teaching proceeds by the rehearsed shape, show it whole once, slowly; break it into its steps; the youngers do each step with the teachers moving among them, one teaching, one watching the room exactly as rehearsed; errors are corrected as information ('the loop goes the other way, like this. Yes, that is it') and never as verdicts. The watching teacher's eyes are the session's management: a child finished early gets the plan's extension, a child stuck gets a teacher beside them, and the room's energy is read like weather and answered by pace, faster where it flags, a breath where it frazzles, because classroom craft is weather work and these ten minutes are the pairs' first honest shift at it.
  4. The youngers' verdict and the close: the session ends when its promise is testable, and it is tested by the children doing the skill unaided, all at once, at their teachers' invitation, the reef knot tied, the crane flown, the rhythm kept and stopped together, while the teachers watch their own promise kept or not kept in other people's hands, which is the moment the record exists for. Applause is given to the youngers, by name as far as the teachers can manage it, for specific things ('you held the rhythm through the hard bar'), and the youngers leave with their work, their adult, and the teachers' thanks. The feedback card is completed by the youngers' adult before anyone disperses and handed to the pairs unread by anyone else first: it is theirs, it is specific, and its three prompts were chosen so that it tells them where their teaching actually landed, from the one professional witness in the room.
  5. The debrief, pairs and cards together: each pair reads its card, then gives its own account in the session's official shape, the promise, whether the youngers' hands kept it, the moment attention was fullest and what the teaching was doing then, the moment it thinned and what they did, and the adult's only marks are questions that return the pairs to their own evidence. The tell-back closes the record: 'You stood in front of real learners with something to give them, and you gave it in their size, at their pace, under their adult's eye, and you watched their hands to find out if it worked. Most people talk about teaching for years before anyone lets them discover that attention cannot be commanded, only earned, minute by minute, by the clarity of what you show. You have the discovery now, at full size, with a card to prove where it landed. Whatever you teach next, a class, a team, a younger sibling at a kitchen table, starts from what your hands learned today.' Plans and cards are filed by pair, as the first documents of a teaching portfolio the pairs may be building longer than they know.

Variations

Easier: A five-minute version: one micro-skill (a knot, a clapping pattern, a fold) taught to a group of four youngers, the full shape (promise, rehearsal, verdict) at the smallest honest size, for a pairs' first teach-back.

Harder: A return version: the same pairs teach the same youngers a second session a month later, building on the first skill. The feedback card's second reading, against the first, is where the pairs can watch their own teaching change, which is the deepest lesson the record offers.

⚠️ Safety

The safeguarding shape is structural and is stated to everyone in advance: the youngers' own adult is present in the room for the entire session and retains all authority over their group. The teen teachers are never alone with the younger children, never responsible for their discipline, toileting, medication, or movement outside the session's room, and any need of that kind is handed to the youngers' adult immediately and kindly. Physical contact is limited to what a younger child initiates (a high-five, a handshake), teachers demonstrate on materials and on their own hands, guide by pointing and by showing again, and never adjust a child's body, hands, or posture. Skills are chosen from the safe shelf for the youngers' actual age: no sharps, no heat, no small parts for the youngest groups, no food, and the teaching pairs' materials are checked against that list by the adult when the plans are approved. A plan that is brilliant and unsafe is rewritten, and the rewriting is presented as the profession working, because it is. The youngers' names and the session's details stay inside the arrangement: no photographs except under the youngers' setting's own ordinary permissions, arranged through their adult, and the feedback card names situations, never children's shortcomings.

🤝 Inclusion notes

Teaching pairs are built for every kind of teacher: the demonstrator and the room-watcher are equal halves of the craft, and a pair can divide them as their strengths run, the partner who watches attention, times the minutes, and moves to the stuck child is doing the work experienced teachers call the real job. Explanations can be given in speech, in gesture and demonstration (the session's preferred channel for young children anyway), with picture-step cards the pair draws for their own plan, or by a partner's voice at the demonstrator's direction, the youngers judge the teaching by whether their hands can do the thing, and every clear channel passes that test at full marks. The debrief's evidence, the promise kept or not, in the youngers' hands, is the same for every pair and is read aloud from the cards, so no pair's standing depends on fluent self-presentation. A pair whose session fails honestly and whose debrief reads the failure precisely has learned the record's deepest content, and the closing protects that truth by naming it in general terms, at full size, where the pair it belongs to can hear it.
