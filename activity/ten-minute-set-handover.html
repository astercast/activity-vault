Ten-Minute Set, Clean Handover

Teen DJs each play a short set and hand the floor to the next DJ without a gap, a clash, or a dead second — the join is the skill.

Ages
13–14
Group
4–20 kids
Time
30–45 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Screen the sets before the session, every track, all the way through: each DJ submits their list in advance and the adult hears each track entire — the session's rule is absolute and is set now, not negotiated mid-set: every lyric is clean enough to be played to the whole room's families, and a track that fails is swapped by its DJ before the day, with the swap treated as programming, not censorship ('a working DJ's whole craft is playing the right record in the room they're actually in'). Set the volume cap with the speaker in the actual room — the level at which a person at the floor's edge can still speak to a partner without shouting — and mark it on the dial or the screen, physically, so the cap survives enthusiasm. Order the DJs and post the running order where the floor can see it; programme the arc of the whole session across the sets (opener's energy, the peak in the middle DJs' hands, a closer who lands the room) and tell each DJ where their set sits in that arc, because a handover is planned from the running order, not improvised at the exchange.

Materials

  • one speaker the room can dance to, run from the adult's device or account (the session's single source — every track comes through it, which is how the volume cap and the lyrics rule are actually enforceable)
  • each DJ's set list, written on paper in advance (three or four tracks, in order, with the planned joining track marked — a set that exists only in a DJ's head cannot be handed over)
  • a visible clock (the set is ten minutes and the handover lands on it — time is an instrument in this record and it is played in the open)
  • floor markers for the DJ position and the handover point (so the change of hands happens at one known place, like a relay exchange zone)

Steps

  1. The framing, at the DJ position, floor gathered: 'A set is a promise to a floor: ten minutes where the music never dies, never clashes, and never wanders off. Today you each play three or four tracks and then you do the hardest thing in the craft — you hand over. The next DJ's first track begins as your last one ends: matched in energy, joined on the beat or on the breath between songs, with both of you at the position, hands ready, like a relay exchange. The floor never notices the join — that is the whole standard. A gap is a dropped baton. A clash is two promises broken at once. And the set you play is the set on your paper: the floor is dancing to a plan, and the plan is what makes the plan's surprises land.' The volume cap and the lyrics rule are stated as the session's constitution, once, without apology: 'The cap is marked. The lists are screened. Inside those two lines, the floor is yours.'
  2. First set, played at full ceremony by the first DJ with the adult alongside for this one alone: the DJ announces nothing — the music does the announcing — and plays their list as written, watching the floor the way the craft requires (heads, feet, the edges where energy leaks) and riding their planned joining track into the exchange zone as the clock runs down. The handover is walked through at full size this first time, narrated quietly by the two DJs themselves: the incoming DJ at the position a full track early, listening, naming the join point to themselves ('on the last chorus — there'), the outgoing DJ's hand leaving the controls as the incoming track takes the floor, both staying at the position until the join has landed and the floor has taken it. Whether it lands clean or rough, the debrief sentence is the same shape: what the join did, in the floor's terms — 'the floor kept moving' or 'the floor checked' — because the floor is the only reviewer this record recognises.
  3. Rotation round: sets proceed down the running order, each DJ arriving at the position during the previous set's penultimate track, set list in hand, and each handover judged by the floor's behaviour and nothing else. DJs learn the round's central discipline in their hands: your last track is not your favourite track — it is the bridge, chosen when the list was written, at the energy the NEXT set needs to start from; a DJ who peaks on their closer has spent the next DJ's opening, and the floor pays for it. The adult's only interventions are the constitution's two lines — a hand drifting above the marked cap is pointed at, silently, once, and the lyrics rule has no second warnings because it was screened, not trusted. Between sets there is no applause break and no announcement: the session is one continuous piece of music with many authors, and it is reviewed as such.
  4. The arc reviewed, mid-session, in two minutes with the music paused exactly once: the DJs gather at the position and read the session so far as one work — where the floor's energy rose, where it sagged, which join carried it and which one cost it — and the remaining DJs may retime (never re-screen) their lists' order within their own sets in light of the evidence, marking the change on their paper in ink so the plan stays a plan. This is the record's programming lesson, and it is stated while the paper is out: 'A set list is a hypothesis. The floor is the experiment. Changing the order because the floor told you something is the craft — changing it because you got bored is noise.'
  5. The closer lands the session: the final DJ plays the room down deliberately — the last track chosen when the list was written to be the session's full stop, energy spent honestly rather than switched off — and the floor is thanked by the DJs collectively, by name and set, in the tell-back's shape: 'Nobody heard a join break. The floor danced through six pairs of hands and the music never died once — that is not six sets, that is one session, and building one thing out of many people's taste, without a seam showing, is the entire job of every DJ, programmer, and host who will ever follow you at it.' Set lists are kept and filed with the running order: a session that landed is programmed again, credited to its DJs, and its joining tracks — the bridges that held — are the first thing next session's DJs study.

Variations

Easier: A two-DJ version: two sets and one handover, rehearsed twice — once walked through narrated, once live — inside twenty-five minutes; the join gets the whole session's attention, which is where a new group's attention belongs.

Harder: A silent-disco version (headphones for the floor, two channels): DJs hand over on one channel while the other runs long — the joins multiply, the floor's channel-switching becomes part of the evidence, and the debrief reads the switches like votes.

⚠️ Safety

Volume is this record's one physical risk and it is governed structurally, not by reminders: the cap is set and marked on the equipment by the adult before the first track, the speaker is positioned away from the floor's ears (never at head height beside the dancers), and a person at the room's edge must be able to hold a conversation without shouting — if they cannot, the level comes down, by the adult's hand, without debate, and the session continues at the cap. All music comes through the single screened source: no personal devices plugged in mid-session, no unscreened requests taken live, because the lyrics rule can only be kept on music that has been heard entire by the adult in advance. The floor itself follows the house's ordinary movement sense — space to move, water available, anybody free to step off the floor at any moment without it being an event; stepping off is listening from the edge, and the DJs are taught to read it as programming information, never as failure.

🤝 Inclusion notes

The DJ position welcomes every kind of musician: a set can be played by a DJ who directs a partner's hands at the controls ('fade on my count — now'), the set list can be built and presented in any format its author works in, and the handover's call can be a raised hand, a tapped shoulder, or a signed count — the exchange zone runs on agreed signals, and agreeing them is part of the pair's planning, written on both lists before the session. The floor is equally open: dancing, moving in a chair, listening from the edge with the programme in hand are all floor behaviour the DJs are taught to read and serve, and the session's arc is programmed for the room that is actually there — a set that lifts a seated floor has done the job exactly as a set that lifts a standing one. The mid-session review is held at the position with every DJ present in whatever way they attend best, and its changes are read aloud from the paper, so no DJ's set depends on another's handwriting.
