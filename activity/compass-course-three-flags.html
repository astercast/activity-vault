Compass Course, Three Flags

Real bearings, counted paces, three flags to find: teen teams set an orienteering course, then run each other's, navigation, not wandering.

Ages 13–14 · 30–50 min · 4 materials

Ages
13–14
Group
4–20 kids
Time
30–50 min
Where
Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Walk the whole ground yourself first, with the session's eyes: the course area must be one bounded, permitted space where every leg can be walked in a straight line without crossing roads, water, other groups' games, or any place a teenager following a needle might not notice arriving, deep mud, a slope that hides a drop, an out-of-bounds fence with a gap in it. Mark the single shared start point where all courses begin. Then calibrate yourself: walk the strip and count your paces over its known length, twice, because you will teach the calibration and a teacher whose own count is vague teaches vagueness. Check every compass against a bearing you know, a fence line, a building edge whose direction you have verified, and set the failures aside without a second thought. Decide the boundaries out loud to yourself in plain words ('nothing beyond the hedge, nothing across the path') so the framing can carry them exactly.

Materials

  • sighting or baseplate compasses, one per team (real instruments, checked by the adult before the session: a compass whose needle sticks or swings lazy is retired, because a course set on a lying compass teaches lying)
  • three flags on poles per course (bright, tall enough to be seen from the bearing's line and no taller. A flag you can see from anywhere makes the bearing pointless)
  • course cards and pencils (each leg written as a bearing and a pace count, nothing else. The card is the whole instruction, and its clarity is the setter's craft)
  • a pace-count calibration strip (a measured 50 paces' worth of ground the adult has taped or paced out in advance, where every navigator learns their own honest pace count before any course is set)

Steps

  1. The framing, at the start point, compasses still in the adult's hands: 'A bearing is a promise your feet keep. This instrument does not know where you are going. It knows one thing, perfectly: which way is which. Today each team sets a course of three flags for another team: three legs, each written as a bearing and a pace count, nothing more. The team that runs your course will find your flags if your numbers are honest and your reading of this ground is true, and they will not find them if either is sloppy, and they will know whose work it was. Then you run theirs. Setters first, runners second, everybody both.' The laws are stated whole: 'Teams stay together, always, inside the boundaries as named. Walking pace on a leg. A compass is read standing still, and running with your eyes on a needle is how ankles and friendships end. If a leg runs you at a boundary, a building, or anywhere the course should not go, stop: a course can be wrong, and reporting that, with your card, is a result. Forcing it is not.'
  2. Calibration round, before any flag is planted: every navigator walks the measured strip and counts their own paces over its known length, twice, and writes their personal count on their course card, because a pace is not a standard unit, it is a property of a pair of legs, and a leg written as '40 paces' means the SETTER's 40 unless the card is walked by the setter's own measure. Teams therefore write legs in metres as well as paces where they can (the strip teaches the conversion on the spot: your count over the known length is your ruler), and a setter who skips calibration has built their course on sand. The round exists so nobody does. The adult watches the strip work like a hawk: counting aloud, head up between counts, eyes returning to the needle only at a standstill.
  3. Setting round: each team walks the ground with its three flags, chooses its three points, each one a place a bearing can honestly reach and a flag can honestly stand: not hidden (this is navigation, not camouflage), not trivial (visible from the start point is not a leg, it is a view), and works backwards from each flag to write the leg that reaches it: the bearing read standing at the previous point with the needle settled, the distance paced and converted. The card is written as the runners will receive it: leg one from the start, leg two from flag one, leg three from flag two, and a finish line that brings the runners back to the start point by a stated bearing, setters walk their own finish leg before they hand the card over, because a course that strands its runners has failed its only examination. Cards are exchanged between teams by the adult, unedited: the setters' handwriting is part of the test, and legibility is a navigation skill.
  4. Running round: teams run the courses they were given, in the order written, at walking pace, compass read at a standstill at each flag before the next leg is begun. The running team's navigator rotates at every flag, the card passes, the compass passes, and the reading is done aloud by the new navigator while the team checks the needle's settling together ('the needle settles before the feet move' is the round's working sentence, said at every leg until it becomes the team's own). A flag that cannot be found gets fifteen honest minutes of re-reading, bearing re-taken from the last certain point, paces re-counted, before it is reported missed, with the card brought back to the adult: a missed flag is a finding about the course, the running, or both, and the debrief will sort it without blame, because both teams know their own card is out there being read the same way.
  5. Debrief at the start point, cards in hand: each course is marked by its runners in the session's official shape, flags found, legs disputed, and the single hardest reading on the card named precisely ('leg two said 140 degrees from the oak; the flag stood at 150 by our needle, one of our instruments or readings was out, and here is our evidence'). Setters answer with their setting notes. Where two compasses disagree, they are checked on the spot against the known bearing from calibration, and the instrument that lied is retired in front of everyone, kindly and permanently. The record's closing lesson, said whole: 'Navigation is a chain of honest numbers. A pace counted carelessly, a needle read while it was still swinging, a bearing written from memory, the chain breaks at its weakest link, and it breaks out there, at walking pace, with the flag in sight of nobody. Today you felt where chains hold. That feeling is the skill.' Flags are collected by their setters, the ground is left as found, and the cards are kept: a well-set course is run again next season, credited to its setters by name.

Variations

Easier: A one-flag version: a single out-and-back leg to one flag and home by the reverse bearing, the whole discipline of calibration, settling, and honest counting inside twenty minutes, for a first session with new instruments.

Harder: A night version (a dark evening session, with the adults' full permission and lit boundaries): the same courses run by torchlight, flags fitted with a reflective band. The needle read by torch held to the side, never waved across a partner's eyes, and every leg suddenly worth twice its daylight price.

⚠️ Safety

Boundaries are the record's first law and they are geographic, not advisory: the course area is one bounded space the adult has walked, teams stay inside it and stay together, and any leg that points a team at a road, water, a car park, or out of the area stops the course on the spot. Reporting a bad leg is a result, forcing it is a breach, and the adult's framing says so before a flag is planted. Compasses are read standing still, always: nobody walks while watching a needle, nobody runs a leg, and the terrain is chosen so that a teenager looking up from a settled reading has nothing to fall off, into, or across within the course's lines. The sun and the session's length are planned like any outdoor record. Water at the start point, a hard finish time, and a whistle signal that means return to the start point now, rehearsed before the setting round so it never needs explaining in the moment. Flags and poles are carried point-down and planted by hand pressure only; a pole that needs force stays unplanted and its point is re-chosen.

🤝 Inclusion notes

A team navigates with every sense it owns: the navigator's job rotates by design, and it divides cleanly, one reads the needle, one counts the paces aloud, one keeps the card and calls the leg, so a navigator who cannot walk the leg can still be the team's needle-reader at each standstill, arriving by the route that suits them and taking the reading the whole team walks on. Bearings can be called in any register, spoken, signed, held up as the card's next line pointed to by its keeper, and the calibration strip teaches every body's own pace honestly, including the pace of wheels over the strip's length, which the setter's conversion treats as exactly what it is: that navigator's ruler, as valid as any pair of legs. Courses are set on ground chosen for step-free lines wherever the site allows, and a leg that cannot be made accessible is re-set by its setters as a matter of craft, not concession. A course only some teams can run is a badly set course, by this record's definition.
