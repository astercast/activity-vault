Big Float–Sink Basin Sort

One big basin, one big object at a time: held over the water, let go — and the basin itself reports, floats or sinks, for the group to sort by.

Ages
3–3
Group
2–8 kids
Time
10–15 min
Where
Indoors & Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Choose the objects the way a scientist chooses specimens: every one big, smooth, washable, and interesting in water — and test every one yourself in the basin first, because the sort only teaches if the basin surprises somebody, and a set where everything floats teaches a lie by accident. You want a honest mix and at least one genuine surprise (the heavy-looking sponge that floats, the small-looking stone that drops like a anchor). The line-up on the tray is the session's order, set by you; objects wait DRY on the tray, because an object tested wet is a different object.

Materials

  • a big wide basin of water, filled to halfway by the grown-up (the laboratory)
  • big test objects, adult-chosen and all far too big for a mouth (a big wooden block, a big sponge, a smooth stone, a big cork or ball, a metal spoon rest, a leaf — six to eight objects, dried and lined up on a tray)
  • two sorting towels beside the basin (the FLOAT towel and the SINK towel — the record's results table)
  • a drying towel for hands and drips

Steps

  1. Set the basin where the whole group can see its surface and say the plain framing: 'Water holds some things up and lets other things fall. Nobody in this room knows which ones yet — the BASIN knows. We are going to ask it, one thing at a time.' State the one rule: 'One object in the water. It goes in from hands held LOW over the middle — let go, never dropped from high, never thrown.' Demonstrate the holder's grip on the first object above the tray: two hands, gentle, like the object is asleep.
  2. First test together, yours: hold the first object low over the middle, say its name, and let go. Whatever it does — floats, sinks, or the comedy third option, floats SIDEWAYS and takes on water — the group says the report in the two official words: 'It FLOATS' or 'It SINKS.' You fish it out, shake it over the basin, and lay it on its results towel: the FLOAT towel or the SINK towel, which the group watches fill like a scoreboard that is also a science table.
  3. Testing round: testers take turns in line order — choose the next object from the tray, carry it in two hands, hold it low over the middle while the group goes quiet (the quiet is ceremony and also how splashes stay small), let go, and call the report first, as the tester whose hands asked the question. Disagreement is settled by the basin, re-shown: 'Watch again.' The basin is the only referee this laboratory recognises, and that is said out loud, kindly, every time it is needed.
  4. Surprise moment, kept for the middle of the round: whichever object you know will fool the room — tested at the point where the group has grown confident — gets its let-go in full ceremony. When the confident call is wrong, the record's line is ready and true: 'The basin fooled EVERYBODY. That is the basin's favourite job.' No tester is ever the fool; the object is the trickster, and the re-test — held, let go, watched together — is demanded by the group itself, because being fooled by water is interesting, not embarrassing.
  5. Finish with the results read back off the towels, the way scientists read a table: 'On the float towel: the block, the sponge, the cork, the leaf. On the sink towel: the stone and the spoon rest. Six things asked, six answers given, and one surprise.' Objects are dried by their testers and returned to the tray in a dry line, the basin is emptied by you, and hands are washed — a laboratory that gets tidied by its scientists is a laboratory that will be trusted again tomorrow.

Variations

Easier: A four-object version: two floaters, two sinkers, no surprise — every tester gets one test and the towels fill evenly inside ten minutes.

Harder: A second-chance round: the sunk objects are dried thoroughly and re-tested after the group discusses whether DRY changes anything — the sponge's honest answer changes, and the discussion of why is the oldest science talk there is.

⚠️ Safety

The basin is the adult's: filled, carried, and emptied by grown-up hands only, set where it cannot be pulled over, and never left with the group unattended for a moment — water at any depth owns the room's full attention while it is out. Objects are big, smooth, and washable, with nothing that can break in water, rust, or come apart into small pieces when wet; an object that cracks, sheds, or rusts is out of the laboratory permanently. Letting go is done low over the middle, always — a splash over the basin's edge wets the floor and the session pauses for the drying towel, because wet floors are this laboratory's one real hazard and it is managed, not dramatised.

🤝 Inclusion notes

The tester's role fits every scientist: an object can be carried on a lap tray to the basin's edge and let go from exactly the height and spot its tester directs — 'lower... middle... NOW' — while a partner's hands follow the direction precisely, because the decision of when is the experiment, and it stays the tester's. The reporter job, calling FLOATS or SINKS for the whole group after each let-go and placing the object on its results towel, is the laboratory's official voice and belongs to whoever holds it, from any station round the basin.
