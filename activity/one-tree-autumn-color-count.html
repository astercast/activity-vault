One Tree Autumn Colour Count

One autumn tree, counted colour by colour with pointing fingers: how many colours is one tree wearing this week?

Before you begin

Ages
3–3
Group
3–12 kids
Time
10–15 min
Where
Outdoors
Prep
No prep needed

Setup

Visit your tree yourself in the same week and count its colours with your own eyes before you promise the group a count: a tree still fully green is a fine tree but a one-colour count, and the record refuses to schedule a rainbow that has not arrived. Choose a tree the group can gather round without standing in a road, a puddle, or anyone's garden, with low branches or fallen leaves at kid eye-height. The colours have to be REACHABLE BY LOOKING, since nothing on this count is touched, picked, or pulled down.

Materials

  • one autumn tree the group can stand under or beside (the adult has visited it this week: its colours are actually turning)
  • no other materials. Eyes and pointing fingers are the instruments

Steps

  1. Gather where the whole tree can be seen and say the plain framing: 'This tree has been wearing green all summer. Now autumn is changing its clothes, a leaf at a time. We are going to count how many colours this one tree is wearing, today, this week, because next week it will be wearing different ones.' State the one rule: 'We count with pointing fingers. Nothing gets picked, pulled, or shaken down. The tree keeps every leaf it still has.'
  2. First colour together: the adult points at the obvious one, the green that is still winning, and the group names it and counts it on one raised finger: 'One: green.' Then hunt the newcomer together: somewhere on a turning tree there is a first yellow, or a first orange, findable if twenty eyes look. The finder points, a second counter must agree they can see it too, and only then does finger two go up: 'Two: yellow.' A colour counts when two counters agree. That agreement rule is the count's whole scientific method, stated at three-year-old size.
  3. Counting stretch: the group works slowly round the tree's colours, yellow, orange, red, brown at the edges, each one pointed at, agreed, and fingered. Expect argument at the borders ('that's not red, that's DARK orange') and treat it as the count working, not failing: the adult records both claims and the group looks again together until the tree itself settles it. A colour only one counter can see stays a CLAIM, held kindly in reserve: 'Maya sees purple. Nobody else can find it yet. The tree may prove her right next week.'
  4. Ground round, the count's second half: fallen leaves under the tree are looked at where they lie, the same counting, at foot height, and new colours sometimes appear down here first (the underside pale, the spotted brown). Leaves on the ground may be pointed at and counted exactly like leaves on the branch; they are not picked up, because the record of the tree's week includes where its leaves have got to, and a picked-up leaf has left the record.
  5. Finish with the count told back as a portrait in numbers: 'This week, our tree is wearing five colours: green still winning, yellow coming, orange at the top, red on one branch, brown beginning underneath. Next week we count again and the tree will have changed its answer.' The recount is promised on the record. A tree counted once is a walk; a tree counted weekly is a season, watched.

Variations

Easier: A two-colour minimum version: on a tree just beginning to turn, count only green and not-green. Every leaf a counter finds that is NOT green gets pointed at and celebrated as the change arriving.

Harder: A leaf-line comparison: counters find the GREENEST leaf they can see and the MOST-TURNED leaf they can see, both by pointing only. The tree's whole season held between two leaves.

Safety

The group gathers where the adult has stood first: no road edge, no slope, no wasp nest or low dead branch over the counting spot. A tree in autumn drops things, and the spot is chosen so what it drops lands on nobody. Nothing is climbed, shaken, or pulled: branches at three are looked at, and a counter who wants a closer look gets the adult's lifted view from the adult's arms, briefly and safely, never a branch bent down by small hands. Fallen leaves stay where they lie and hands that have touched the ground get washed back inside. The count is eyes-only partly so the washing stays simple.

Inclusion notes

Pointing is the only instrument, so the count is open at every height and from every seat: a counter stationed at the tree's best vantage in whatever seat they use has the same tree, the same colours, and the same deciding vote. A colour they call still needs its second agreer, exactly like everyone else's. Calls arrive as words, points, or a sound the group learns means 'THERE. That one'; the adult checks every call against the tree the same way. The count-keeper job, holding up the running fingers where all can see them, belongs to whoever holds it and is the expedition's official record.
