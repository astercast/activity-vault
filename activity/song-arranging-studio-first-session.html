Song Arranging Studio, First Session

Teens take one permitted song apart, melody, chords, structure, and rebuild it in a new arrangement the whole room rehearses and logs.

Before you begin

Ages
13–14
Group
6–24 kids
Time
50–60 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Clear the song before you clear the room: choose a song the studio is entitled to rearrange, learn it entire yourself, melody, chord pattern, section order, and write its skeleton on the board in advance, in the plain shapes the framing will use: verse, chorus, bridge if it has one, and the chord loop under each. Take the room's instrument census honestly at setup and list what actually sounds: a keyboard with a dead octave, three shakers and fourteen voices is an orchestra with a dead octave, and the arrangement will be built for the room that exists. Set the studio's shape now and keep it: the song straight first, the skeleton mapped second, one change at a time third, a full arranged run last. An arranging studio that changes everything at once learns nothing, because nobody can say which change did the work. Agree the decision law before opinions arrive and write it on the log's first page: a change is tried entire, heard entire, and kept or cut by the studio's vote after one full hearing, with the arranger of the moment holding a casting voice only on ties, taste argued once, heard once, settled.

Materials

  • one song the studio may honestly use, chosen and cleared before the session (a public-domain song, a traditional song, or a song the adult has permission to teach and rearrange. The song's words and melody written out on the room's board, entire, because an arrangement built on a half-remembered song is a version of a rumour)
  • whatever instruments and sound-makers the room truly has, gathered and tested before the studio opens (voices count first. A studio that can only arrange for instruments it wishes it had will arrange nothing; every sound in the room is listed on the board as the studio's real orchestra)
  • the arrangement board and the version log (the board carries the song's skeleton, sections, chord pattern, who does what, in the order the version runs; the log, one page per version, records what was changed, what it did, and what the studio decided, kept by the session's keeper)
  • a pitch and a pulse the whole room can find (a starting note given the same way every run, and the tempo set by a count-in the arranger of the moment gives aloud. Versions are compared at agreed tempos first, so a change of speed is a decision, never a drift)

Steps

  1. The framing, the song's skeleton on the board, before anything is changed: 'An arrangement is an argument about what the song is. The songwriter gave us a melody, a set of chords and an order of sections. Everything else, how fast it breathes, what it is played on, which section arrives like a surprise, where the room sings and where one voice stands alone, is the arrangement, and today it is ours. We will not argue in the abstract. We will sing the song straight first, until the room owns it. Then we change ONE thing, we hear the whole song with that change, and the log records what the change did, not whether it was clever, what it did: the chorus stood taller, the verse went thin, the ending stopped landing. A version is kept because the room heard it work, and cut because the room heard it fail, and both verdicts go in the same ink, because next session's studio starts from this log, not from somebody's memory of a good idea.' The song is sung straight, entire, at the agreed tempo, and the skeleton on the board is corrected in the open if the singing finds it wrong, the board serves the song, not the other way round.
  2. Mapping the skeleton, the studio's analytical middle: the room walks the song section by section and names what each part is doing, where the melody climbs, where the chords loop and where they break, the one moment the whole song leans on, with the keeper entering the map in the log as it is agreed. Instruments are auditioned against the skeleton, not in the abstract: each sound in the room's orchestra is tried on the job the studio is considering for it, carrying the pulse, answering the melody, filling the chorus floor, and a sound that cannot hold its job at the room's honest volume is reassigned without disgrace, because arranging is casting, and a miscast shaker is the arranger's error, not the shaker's. Singers map the voice work the same way: where the room sings together, where a section drops to a hum under a single line, where silence, a full bar of it, counted, does more than any voice could. The map is read back entire by the keeper before the first change is tried, so the studio changes a song it has agreed it understands.
  3. One change at a time, the studio's discipline and its pleasure: the arranger of the moment, a role that rotates by the log's order, open to every member, proposes exactly one change, states what they expect it to do in one sentence, and the keeper enters the prediction before a note sounds, because a studio that predicts in writing learns twice as fast as one that only reacts. The song runs entire with the change. Then the studio judges by the framing's standard: the prediction read back, the room's verdict heard, the change kept, cut, or amended once and re-heard, and amended once means once, because a version polished by nine amendments in one session is a new song nobody voted on. Changes the room has genuinely tried in studios like this, named so first-timers can reach for them: slow the chorus and let the verse run; move the strongest section to the front and pay for it at the ending; strip the instruments out under the bridge so the voices stand alone; trade the pulse from hands to feet; sing the final chorus a step higher only if the room can truly stand there, each tried entire, predicted, logged.
  4. The arranged run and the recording of the version, the session's summit: when the studio has kept its changes, usually fewer than the room first wanted, which the keeper notes kindly, because restraint is an arranging skill, the song runs entire in its new arrangement, standing, at performance intention, with the arranger of the final version conducting the count-in and the stops. If the site records, the adult runs one take of the full run and the log notes it; if not, the log's version page is the record, and it is written to be re-performed from: section order, tempo, who carries what, the kept changes in order, and the one change the studio cut that a future session should retry, named with its prediction, because a cut change with an honest prediction is an inheritance, not a failure. Singers and players sign the version page as arrangers, all of them, because in this studio the arrangement belongs to the room that argued it into shape.
  5. Closing the studio, the straight song sung once more, on purpose: the room sings the song as it arrived, original tempo, original order, nobody's arrangement, and hears it with arranged ears, which is the session's real graduation. The keeper reads the log back entire: the map, the predictions, the verdicts, the final version's page. The tell-back is said with the board still carrying both skeletons, the song's and the studio's: 'This morning this song had one shape, and it was somebody else's. It has two now. We took it apart without breaking it, we changed one thing at a time and wrote down what each change did, we kept what the room heard working and we cut what it heard failing, and both kinds of hearing are in the log in the same hand. An arrangement is an argument about what the song is. Ours is in the book, signed, and ready to be argued with by the next studio. Which is exactly what arrangements are for.' The board is photographed or copied into the log before it is wiped, the instruments are counted back by their players, and the log is stored with the song's card where the next studio will find the whole argument in one place.

Variations

Easier: A voices-only version (a room with no instruments to speak of): the whole studio run on the room's first orchestra, voices, hands, feet and silence, with tempo, order, humming floors and the counted bar of silence carrying every change the session tries.

Harder: A two-song version (a returning studio): the same skeleton method run on a second cleared song in a contrasting shape, the two version pages compared in the log, and the studio's one transferable law, the change that worked on both, written as the book's standing advice.

Safety

Volume is this studio's real hazard and it is governed like one: the room's working volume is set by the adult at the framing and marked in the log, a run that has to be shouted over is stopped and reset quieter as a craft correction, and if ears are ringing, the room was too loud. That sentence is in the log's safety page and any member may say it at any time and be thanked, because hearing is the studio's instrument and nobody spends it for a chorus. Amplification, where the site uses any, is adult-set and adult-adjusted; nobody rides a volume control mid-run. Instruments are handled as the room's tools: strings, sticks and shakers stay in their players' space, a borrowed instrument is returned to its owner's hands, and the instrument census at setup includes the honest exclusions, a cracked drum, a frayed cable, taken out of the orchestra before enthusiasm can volunteer them. Song choice is a safeguarding decision as much as a musical one: words are read entire by the adult in advance, anything the room cannot sing in front of its own families does not enter the studio, and a member who will not sing a line on conscience or comfort hums, plays, or keeps the log at full standing, without explanation owed to anyone.

Inclusion notes

An arranging studio is built out of doors other rooms do not have: the keeper's chair, predictions entered before they are heard, verdicts in the same ink, the version page written to be re-performed from, is seated work at the studio's centre, and the arranger rotation, the session's highest honour, is an office of judgement and one stated sentence, open in whatever register its holder commands: a change can be proposed in speech, in writing on the board, or by demonstrating it on one instrument or one hummed line. Singers join at the volume and in the part their voice honestly has; the voice map is built so humming floors, counted silences and pulse parts are real arranging materials with real names in the log, not consolations, and a member may hold the pulse for the whole arranged run and be, in the version page's credits, its foundation. Instruments are assigned by the census and the job, with seated positions woven into the room's shape as ordinary furniture; tempos are agreed and counted in, never sprung, and every run's count-in is given in sight as well as sound. Nobody auditions, nobody solos without asking, and the log credits the whole room as arrangers. The studio's way of keeping its authorship as wide in fact as in the framing.
