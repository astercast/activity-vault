Personal Map Introductions

New teens draw a map of the places that made them, real, remembered, or hoped for, and introduce themselves by giving the room a tour.

Before you begin

Ages
13–14
Group
6–24 kids
Time
40–55 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Draw the legend before you invite a single map, and draw it where the whole room can keep it in sight: the four shared symbols, each named in the plainest words the room owns, with the session's two laws read aloud as part of the setup itself. Every map is its maker's to censor, and every tour is three stations, no more. Choose the paper and the time honestly: a map drawn in four frantic minutes is a doodle with a legend, so the drawing is given the session's longest unbroken stretch and the host protects it like a reading room, music low or none, talk in the pencil register, nobody's country commented on while it is still being made. Set the tour order at setup by the slate's rotation, guides volunteering for early slots if they wish, because a tour is a kindness done at speaking pace and the order should never be a popularity instrument. Prepare the room's reception law with the same care as its drawing law: listeners tour as guests. Questions at a tour's end are invitations a guide may accept or pass entire, nobody photographs a map without its maker's written-on-the-slate consent, and every map leaves the room in its maker's hands unless the maker freely gives it to the session's folder.

Materials

  • large plain paper and the drawing kit the room trusts. Pencils, colour for those who want it, rulers strictly optional (a personal map is not a test of draughtsmanship: a labelled box with a river through it is a complete country, and the session states that law before a line is drawn, because the map's job is introduction, not cartography)
  • the legend cards. The map's agreed symbols, kept deliberately few: a star for where I am known, a road for a journey that changed me, water for where I am calm, a door for a place I mean to reach (members may invent one symbol of their own and must then teach it, because a legend with one personal entry is this record's signature: the room learns each map partly in a shared language and partly in its maker's)
  • the tour slate. The session's order of guides, kept by the host: each tour's country, its guide, and its three stations (the slate's law is three: every guide chooses exactly three places to present, so a tour is an introduction, not an autobiography, and every country, however rich, is toured at the same honest length)
  • the blank-country law, stated at setup and printed on every legend card: any region of any map may be left blank, unlabelled and unasked-about, by its maker's whole right. No reason, no apology, no tour stop (the law is the record's foundation: a map its maker cannot censor is not an introduction, it is an interrogation with pencils)

Steps

  1. The framing, legend cards up, paper still blank: 'Draw the country only you know. Every one of us is from somewhere, not just a town, a whole geography: the kitchen where your name is said the way you like it, the road that taught you something, the water where your head goes quiet, the door you have not walked through yet and mean to. Today each of us draws that country as a map. There is a legend, and it is shared, a star where you are known, a road for the journey that changed you, water for calm, a door for where you are headed, and one symbol that is yours alone, which you will teach us when your tour comes. Then each guide gives the room a tour: three stations, chosen by you, at a speaking pace, an introduction, not an autobiography. And the law that makes all of this safe enough to be honest: any part of your country may stay blank. Unlabelled. Unexplained. Blank is a complete answer on a map, and in this room nobody, nobody, asks what a blank country is hiding. It is not hiding. It is yours.' The legend is read symbol by symbol, one invented-symbol example is drawn by the host on the board, theirs, honestly, and the drawing begins in the session's protected quiet.
  2. The drawing, the session's long protected middle: maps grow at their makers' own geography, some dense as cities, some three places and a river, and the host walks the room as a keeper of conditions rather than a critic: paper for the member whose country outgrew its sheet, time warnings given gently and early, and the blank-country law kept in practice, which means the host's own curiosity is the first thing disciplined. No 'what's that going to be?' over any shoulder, ever. Members who finish early take the legend's second job: drawing the session's shared compass rose for the folder, north invented by the room, since a compass for countries like these points at something better than north, while slower countries are given the session's full grace, because a map hurried at the end is a country with its last county amputated, and the slate's tours can begin around a table where one map is still being drawn if its maker consents to the company.
  3. The tours, three stations each, run from the slate: a guide stands or sits with their map held, pinned or laid as they choose, and walks the room through their three stations in their chosen order, the star, the road, the water, the door, the invented symbol taught when its station comes, at a pace the host guards: a tour is finished when its three stations are given, applause is the room's, and questions follow the reception law entire, accepted or passed without a flicker. Listeners' duty is cartographic, not conversational: each listener notes, on their own tour card, one place from each country they would visit and why in four words or fewer. A discipline that keeps attention on the maps and gives every guide written evidence of having been genuinely toured, which the guides collect at the session's end. A guide may tour a map drawn sparse, drawn fictional-leaning, drawn almost entirely of doors, the slate records the country and its three stations without ranking, because this record's introductions are not entries in a contest of interesting lives.
  4. The atlas round, the session's closing geography: with its makers' consent, consent asked map by map, a no entered on the slate as a complete and respected answer, the maps are laid together on the long table as one atlas of the room, and the crew reads the shared country aloud: the waters are many, the doors face interestingly different directions, the journeys cluster and scatter, patterns spoken of with delight and without a single comparison of worth, because an atlas is not a league table. Guides reclaim their maps from the atlas in slate order, collecting their listeners' tour cards as they go. The session's only souvenir, and it belongs to the guide entire. Maps freely given to the session's folder are registered on the slate with their makers' names and the folder is stored where the room's next session can be introduced to this one, because that is what an atlas is for: the next new country will not be the only stranger at the table.
  5. Closing the session, the legend taken down last: the host reads the slate back entire, countries toured, stations given, maps foldered by consent and maps taken home, in the same tone for both, and the room's compass rose, if the early finishers made one, is offered to the folder or to the room's wall by the crew's vote. The tell-back is said with the blank legend cards still on the table: 'This morning we were names on a register. Now we are countries. We drew the places that made us, the kitchens and roads, the waters and the doors, we taught each other one symbol nobody else has, we toured three stations apiece like guides who respect their own borders, and the blank parts of our maps stayed blank, unasked, entire. Draw the country only you know. This room has seen it now, the parts you chose to show, and a country that has been toured is not a stranger's anymore.' Maps leave in their makers' hands or into the folder by the makers' word, tour cards go to their guides, and the slate is filed with the folder where the atlas's next session will find the whole geography in one place.

Variations

Easier: A one-symbol version (a very short session or a very new room): every map is a single place, the star alone, drawn large, toured in one station each, with the full legend and blank-country law taught entire, ready to grow countries next time.

Harder: A hopes-only version (a room where present geography is tender): every map is drawn in doors, places meant to be reached, journeys planned, waters not yet found, because a country made entirely of intentions is still a true introduction, and sometimes the most honest one in the room.

Safety

The blank-country law is this record's safety system and it admits no exceptions: no member is ever asked, by the host, by a listener, by a question card, by a joke, to explain, label or apologise for an unlabelled region, a missing home place, a country drawn fictional, or a tour that passes a station by; the host retires any question that reaches for a blank, kindly and on the spot, and the retirement is final and unanalysed. Maps are personal documents and are handled as such: they are never photographed, scanned, pinned up or passed around without their maker's explicit consent for that exact use, they leave in their makers' hands by default, and a map left behind by accident is foldered unread by the host and returned in private. The session's curiosity is never an excuse written afterwards. Content follows the room's standing care: a map may hold a place of grief or difficulty entirely lawfully, its maker tours only what they choose, and the host's attention to a quiet guide is one neutral offer of a pass, made once. A passed tour is entered on the slate without commentary and the session moves on with genuine warmth, because the right to be unintroduced is the foundation every real introduction in this room stands on. Drawing tools are the room's ordinary safe kit; the session's real hazards are social, and they are governed by law, rehearsal and the host's early, kind interventions, in that order.

Inclusion notes

A map introduction is built from doors other introductions do not have: the whole performance can be drawn and toured from a seat, a map can be toured by its maker in speech, in writing read aloud by the host at the maker's dictation, or by the maker's own finger travelling the stations in silence while the host names each legend symbol as it is reached. A silent tour is a complete tour, and the slate records it in the same ink. Drawing is judged by no standard but legibility to its own legend: labelled boxes, dictated maps drawn by a partner's hand under the maker's direction, maps in a single colour or in relief with raised lines. All are countries entire, and the partner-scribe, where one serves, holds an honoured office credited on the slate, because the country is the maker's even when the pencil was borrowed. Listeners' tour cards can be written, drawn or dictated, the atlas round is read aloud by the host so its patterns reach every member, and consent for the atlas and the folder is asked in a form every member can answer privately. A card turned face down is a no, counted without a glance. Nobody's introduction depends on disclosing: the blank-country law is restated at every stage, and a map consisting of one door and three blank counties is toured, applauded and filed, where its maker wishes, with exactly the ceremony of the densest atlas page in the room.
