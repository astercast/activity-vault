The Story of the Lights: Ramayana Sequencing

A prince exiled, a long road home, and lamps lit to guide him. Hear the Diwali story and put its pictures in order.

Ages 6–11 · 25–35 min · 3 materials

Ages
6–11
Group
4–28 kids
Time
25–35 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Find a good published children's retelling of the Ramayana at the library if you can. Make the cards yourself, one beat per card: Prince Rama and Sita; the exile to the forest; Ravana carries Sita away; Hanuman and the allies search; the battle, and good wins; the long journey home; the people of Ayodhya light rows of diyas to guide them; the celebration.

Materials

  • teacher-made picture cards (6-8 scenes)
  • paper (one sheet per kid)
  • crayons or markers

Steps

  1. Tell the story once, slowly, holding each card up at its beat, then laying it face down. Eight beats, no rushing. This is one of the oldest stories in the world and it can hold a room.
  2. Pairs get the full shuffled set face up. Put the story back in order, arguing from what you remember. The argument is the comprehension check.
  3. Retell it to your partner using the cards as your script, one sentence per card. Partners may correct the order, kindly, by pointing at the card.
  4. Check the sequence as a class against the telling. Then say the ending out loud together: the people lit lamps to guide Rama home. That is why rows of diyas line doorways and windowsills at Diwali.
  5. Close by drawing your favorite scene on the back of one card. The set goes in an envelope for the next class to sequence cold.

Variations

Easier: Five cards only, and the adult retells while kids hold each card up as its beat arrives.

Harder: Write a one-sentence caption per card in your own words and bind the set into a class book, with the storyteller credited on the cover page.

🤝 Inclusion notes

The Ramayana is a sacred Hindu epic, thousands of years old. Tell a published retelling straight, name it as Hindu, and never act it out in costume or turn its figures into cartoons. Different families and regions mark Diwali with different stories; if a kid offers theirs, that is a contribution, not a correction.
