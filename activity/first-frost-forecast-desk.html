First Frost Forecast Desk

Teens run a frost forecast for real gardeners: evening readings, a signed prediction, and a morning check that grades it honestly.

Ages 13–14 · 25–40 min · 4 materials

Ages
13–14
Group
3–12 kids
Time
25–40 min
Where
Indoors & Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Set the thermometer before the season's first cold nights are due, at the height frost actually strikes, down among the beds, not up at eye level on a warm wall, and read it yourself for three evenings running to learn what its numbers mean against the mornings that follow, because a desk that has not calibrated its own instrument will sign forecasts it does not understand. Agree with the gardeners, in person, what the board is for: the desk forecasts the overnight minimum at the beds and calls FROST or NO FROST by a stated hour each evening; the gardeners decide what to cover; the desk never touches another grower's plants. Choose that hour so the forecast is usable, early enough to cover by, late enough that the evening's evidence is in, and fix the season's span in the ledger now: first forecast date, last forecast date, and the rule that the desk publishes every night in between, cold-looking or mild, because a forecast desk that only appears when drama threatens is a fair-weather friend with a thermometer. Prepare the board where gardeners actually pass, with the signature line at its foot: every forecast is signed by the forecaster who issued it.

Materials

  • a minimum thermometer at bed height in the garden or growing area (the desk's instrument. Read at dusk, reset after reading, its overnight minimum is the number the morning check will grade)
  • the desk's sources (an adult-checked weather service forecast for the site, looked at together each evening. The desk uses it as one witness among several, never as the answer)
  • the forecast board and ledger (the board where tonight's signed forecast is posted for the gardeners; the ledger where forecasts, evidence and morning verdicts accumulate across the frost season)
  • fleece and covers, ready folded by the beds (the forecast has a purpose: when the desk calls frost, the gardeners who read the board can act on it. A forecast nobody can use is a diary)

Steps

  1. The framing, at the board, on the season's first evening: 'A forecast is a promise you check in the morning. This desk does not predict the weather in general. It answers one question for the people who grow here: will frost touch the beds tonight, and how cold will the coldest hour be? We answer it from evidence: the thermometer's own record, the sky at dusk, the wind or its absence, and the official forecast read as one witness, cross-examined like the others. Then we sign it. Tomorrow morning, before anything else, the desk reads the minimum the night actually left and grades its own forecast in the ledger, right, wrong, or lucky, and lucky is a grade, and it is not a compliment. Gardeners will cover real plants on our word. That is the weight this board carries, and it is why the signature is on it.' The first evening's evidence is gathered and the first forecast signed at full ceremony, however mild the night looks. The desk's habits are built on the easy nights so they hold on the hard ones.
  2. The evening round, run the same way all season so the forecasts stay comparable: the thermometer is read from last night and reset; tonight's witnesses are taken in order. The sky (clear skies let the ground's heat escape; cloud is a blanket and the desk says which it sees), the wind (moving air mixes the cold away from the beds; a still, clear dusk is the frost's favourite weather and the round names it exactly so), the feel of the air at bed height compared with the desk's own record of similar evenings in the ledger, and the official forecast, read aloud and minuted with its number, neither worshipped nor ignored. The forecaster on duty weighs the witnesses in the open, in front of the desk, and states the call and the predicted minimum as one signed sentence on the board: a call that cannot be written as a number and a word, FROST or NO FROST, has not been made yet.
  3. Disagreement round, used whenever the witnesses argue: when the official number says safe and the dusk says danger (or the reverse), the desk does not average its witnesses into mush, it names the disagreement on the board under the forecast ('official minimum 4 degrees; desk calls frost in the hollow beds, clear, still dusk and our thermometer's record'), because gardeners deserve the argument, not just the verdict, and because the morning grade is twice as instructive when the losing witness is on record. The desk also grades by place, not just by night: frost is a local animal that settles in hollows and spares slopes, so the ledger keeps its verdicts bed by bed where the thermometer's rounds allow a second reading. A forecast can be right for the top beds and wrong for the low ones on the same night, and a desk that learns its garden's cold corners is worth more each week of the season.
  4. The morning check, the desk's conscience, done before the board is touched: the minimum thermometer is read first, last night's coldest truth at bed height, and the verdict is entered in the ledger against the signed forecast in the season's official shape: RIGHT (the call and the number both held), WRONG (either failed), or LUCKY (the call held for reasons the evidence did not contain, a cloud bank that arrived unscripted at midnight), with lucky recorded at exactly the same size as right, because a desk that hides its luck will start believing its own legend by November. The board is then cleared and the streak kept honestly at its head, forecasts issued, calls right, where every gardener can watch the desk's record grow at the same speed as its reputation, and never faster.
  5. Season's end, the audit evening: when the last forecast date passes, the desk reads its whole ledger aloud to the gardeners, the record entire, the best call of the season and the evidence that carried it, the worst miss and what it taught, the hollow beds' final tally against the slope's, and hands the garden its closing page: the first frost's actual date, the coldest night's actual minimum, and the desk's one earned instruction to next year's forecasters, written by the people the mornings graded. The tell-back closes the season at the board: 'We promised the garden a checked forecast every night, and the mornings checked us back. Some nights we were right, some nights we were wrong, and every one of them is in the book in the same handwriting. A forecast you never grade is a guess with a signature on it. Ours were graded. That is the whole desk.' The thermometer is cleaned, the board stored with the ledger, and the fleece stays folded for the gardeners, whose beds the desk served and never touched.

Variations

Easier: A two-week version (a short cold snap expected): the desk runs for one fortnight around the likeliest frost dates, the full round, signed forecasts and morning grades, compressed into the nights that matter most.

Harder: A classroom-window version (no beds to serve): the desk forecasts for a row of tender plants on an outdoor sill the group itself grows. The gardeners are the class, the covers are real, and a miss costs the desk its own seedlings, which is the honest tuition.

⚠️ Safety

Evening rounds run in the season's real dark and cold, and are planned like it: the route to the thermometer is walked in daylight first, torches are carried by the reader and a partner, nobody goes to the beds alone, and the desk's hour is early enough that the round is a task, not an expedition. Cold is dressed for, not endured as a point of pride. The round is twenty minutes of standing readings, and a forecaster who is shivering reads nothing accurately, including themselves. The thermometer is handled as glass instruments are handled, over the beds and never over paths, and if it is a spirit thermometer it is still treated as fragile kit; a breakage is reported to the adults the same evening, not discovered by the morning check. The desk advises; it never acts on other people's plants. Covers are lifted and laid by the gardeners who own the beds, because a forecast desk that starts moving other growers' fleece has mistaken its authority, and the boundary is stated in the framing before the first forecast is signed.

🤝 Inclusion notes

Forecasting is a desk of many chairs by design: the evening round needs a thermometer reader, a sky witness, a wind witness, a keeper of the ledger and the forecaster who signs, and the signing, the desk's highest honour, belongs to whoever weighed the evidence best that week, not to whoever walked furthest to gather it. Readings can be called from the garden path while a partner reads at the beds; evidence can be given in any register, spoken, written on the round's slate, pointed to on the ledger's own comparison pages, and the morning verdict is read aloud in full at the board, so no member's season depends on small print in cold hands. The rota is published for the whole season ahead, pairs are fixed so nobody rounds alone, and a forecaster who cannot make an evening hands the witnesses' slate to their pair with the desk's blessing recorded in the book: the forecast is the desk's, whoever the dusk sends.
