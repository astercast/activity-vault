Big Feelings Card Show and Say

One big feelings card at a time, drawn and held high: name the feeling, try the face, and the whole circle tries it back — every feeling a right answer.

Ages
3–3
Group
4–16 kids
Time
10–15 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Draw the cards yourself, big and unmistakable: one face per card, features a room away can read — a smile you could land a plane on, a sleepy face with its eyes all but shut. Keep the set honest and small: six feelings a three-year-old actually has before lunch. Practise your own faces in a mirror without embarrassment, because you will be making all six in front of an audience shortly, at full size — an adult willing to do a genuinely surprised face, eyebrows to the hairline, is this activity's whole licence, and the circle will calibrate its courage on yours.

Materials

  • big feelings cards, adult-drawn (a hand-sized-plus card per feeling — happy, sad, surprised, angry, sleepy, silly — ONE big simple face on each, drawn bold, no words needed)
  • no other materials — the circle's own faces are the second set of cards

Steps

  1. Hold the fanned cards where the circle can see them and say the plain framing: 'Faces tell feelings. These cards have big feelings on them. One person at a time draws a card, holds it high, and we all find out which feeling it is — together.' State the one rule, which is the activity's entire constitution: 'Every feeling is a right answer. There is no wrong feeling on a card, and no wrong feeling on a face, ever.' Draw the first card yourself, rigged kindly by fate and your own ordering.
  2. First card together, yours: hold it high — HAPPY, say — and do the whole ceremony at full size so the circle can copy a complete turn: name it ('This face is HAPPY'), try the face (your biggest honest smile, held a real second), and say one true small thing the feeling fits ('Happy is how I feel when the sun is out at recess'). Then the circle tries the face back at you, all together — twenty happy faces at once is a sight that teaches the turn better than any explaining: show, name, try, share, be mirrored.
  3. Drawing round: drawers take a card in turn order, hold it high themselves, and the NAMING is shared work — the drawer names it if they can, the circle helps if they reach out, and the adult's naming is the last resort, not the first. The drawer tries the face (any size — a small sad mouth is a complete sad face at three), offers their fitting thing if they have one — a drawer may pass the sentence and keep the face, which counts in full — and the circle mirrors the drawer exactly: their size, their version, faithfully. Being copied kindly by a whole circle is the activity's payload, and every drawer gets it identical.
  4. Tricky-card moment, taken seriously: when ANGRY or SAD is drawn, the room sometimes giggles from surprise. The constitution covers it, restated gently and once: 'Angry is a right answer. Sad is a right answer. The circle copies the face kindly, because one day the face on the card is the face somebody is actually wearing.' Then model the copy yourself at full respect — a proper angry face, arms uncrossed, no comedy — and watch the circle's copy change quality in front of you. That change is the most important thing that will happen all week in this room, and it happens inside a card game.
  5. Finish with the whole set held up one by one by their drawers in a feelings line — every card, every face, every drawer standing with the feeling they drew — while the group names the line end to end: 'Happy, sleepy, surprised, sad, silly, angry. Six feelings, and every one of them belongs in this room.' Cards are collected face by face into your hands, and the last sentence is the constitution again, because constitutions bear repeating: 'Whatever feeling you arrive wearing tomorrow is a right answer too.'

Variations

Easier: A three-card set (happy, sad, sleepy) and a small circle: every drawer gets two turns inside ten minutes and the mirroring ceremony stays complete.

Harder: A feeling-of-the-moment round: a drawer draws a card and the circle must find, in the room or the day so far, one real thing that matches it — 'surprised: when the umbrella opened indoors!' — the game grows a memory.

⚠️ Safety

The constitution — every feeling is a right answer — is a safety rule before it is a kind one: no drawer is ever teased, corrected, or laughed at for a feeling they draw, show, or arrive wearing, and the adult's enforcement of that is instant, warm, and public, because the circle is learning what this room does with feelings. Faces are tried at the size their owners choose; nobody is ever required to make a face, say a sentence, or be mirrored — a drawer may hold the card while the adult makes the face for the circle to copy, and the turn is the drawer's in full. Cards are big, single-faced, and plain — nothing small to chew, nothing laminated sharp at the corners; corners get rounded by the adult's scissors before the set's first circle.

🤝 Inclusion notes

The ceremony needs no spoken word and no particular face: a drawer can name by pointing at the card they hold (the card names itself), try the feeling at whatever size their face offers today, and be mirrored for exactly that — the circle copies what IS shown, faithfully, which is the whole promise. A drawer who directs the turn — choosing which card the adult draws next, calling the circle's mirror moment — has hosted the activity in full. Feelings can also be answered with a whole body (sleepy shoulders, surprised arms), and the circle mirrors bodies as faithfully as faces.
