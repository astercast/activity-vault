Overwintering Garlic Planting Crew

Teens plant garlic in autumn for a summer harvest: cloves set pointed-up under mulch, the winter logged honestly, spring emergence counted shoot by shoot.

Ages 13–14 · 40–55 min · 4 materials

Ages
13–14
Group
4–16 kids
Time
40–55 min
Where
Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Choose the variety before you promise the bed anything, and choose it from evidence: work with the adults to pick a garlic suited to the site's winters from a trusted supplier's description, and copy that description's planting and harvest guidance onto the bed book's first page word for word, because the crew's whole discipline is that the timetable comes from the crop's own instructions and the site's own frost dates, never from a crew member's optimism. Walk the ground on a dry day and test it the way growers test it: dig one trowel-depth, take a handful, and squeeze. A bed that holds a wet ball will rot cloves in a wet winter, and a drained bed a short walk away is worth more than a convenient swamp. Agree the bed's tenancy in writing now, across the whole cycle, autumn planting, winter silence, spring emergence, summer harvest, with named understudies for the spring counts, because a crop planted by one term's crew and counted by nobody is a lesson in exactly the wrong thing. Set the bed book up before a clove is broken: the map page, the planting-day page, the winter log, and the spring count's empty columns, so the bed is a record from its first row, not a memory reconstructed at harvest.

Materials

  • seed garlic from a trusted supplier or a known grower's stock, bought as whole bulbs and broken into cloves on planting day (firm, heavy bulbs with tight skins. The crew rejects any clove that is soft, mouldy or shrivelled, because the bed's year begins with what goes into it)
  • a real bed or a deep, drained growing plot agreed with its owner for the whole cycle, autumn to the following summer (the crew's founding agreement, in writing: whose ground, who waters in a dry spring, and who is still responsible when the harvest term arrives)
  • planting tools the site already keeps (trowels or dibbers for the holes, a line and pegs for straight rows, labels that survive weather, and the bed book, a hardback ledger holding the planting map, the variety, the source and every count the bed will ever earn)
  • mulch for the winter blanket (clean straw, shredded leaves or the site's own agreed covering, in enough depth to matter. A scatter is a gesture, a blanket is a measured depth the book records)

Steps

  1. Planting day, the framing said at the bed's edge, bulbs still whole in the basket: 'You plant for a harvest you will hand to summer. Nothing else in this archive asks you to work for a season you will not be in charge of, and that is why this bed matters. Garlic goes in backwards to every other crop you know: planted in autumn, rooted before the hard cold, silent all winter under its blanket, and only in spring does it tell you what the winter did. Your job today is small and exact, one clove, pointed end up, at the depth and spacing the book states, in a row the line keeps straight, and your job after today is smaller and harder: log the winter, count what emerges, and never claim a shoot you did not see. A bed planted carelessly in October is a disappointment discovered in April, when nothing can be replanted. So we plant like people who intend to be believed in spring.' Bulbs are broken at the bed, never earlier, a clove separated days ahead dries at its root plate, and each clove is inspected as it is separated, firm ones to the planting tray, rejects to the book's count, in the open.
  2. The planting, row by row, at the book's stated depth and spacing: the line is pegged, the holes are made to a marked depth, the crew uses a dibber or trowel shaft marked with tape at the book's depth, so depth is measured, not estimated, and each clove is set pointed end up, its flat root plate down, covered, and firmed once by hand. A pointed-end-down clove will still try to grow, bent and weakened, and the framing names that honestly: orientation is not fussiness, it is the plant's geometry, and a planter who finds their own mistake re-opens that one hole and sets it right before the row moves on. The map page is drawn as the rows are planted, variety by variety where the bed holds more than one, with the row labels hammered in at the row ends in letters the winter cannot wash off. A map and its labels are the same fact in two places, and the book-keeper reads each row back against the bed before the mulch goes down.
  3. The blanket and the winter log, the crew's long quiet job: mulch is laid to the book's measured depth across the whole bed once the ground has taken its first real cold, laid too early it shelters everything the grower does not want sheltered, and the book records the date, the depth and the weather it was laid in. Then the bed is left alone on purpose, and the winter log is kept monthly and briefly by the rota: mulch still in place or lifted by wind and re-laid, standing water after storms noted and its draining watched, any heave, a clove lifted by freeze and thaw, sitting proud of the soil, pressed gently back by an adult-guided hand and entered in the log with its row. The log's honesty rule is the bed's whole ethic: a month nobody visited is entered as a month nobody visited. The winter is the crop's, not the crew's performance, and a truthful gap in the log teaches more than a fluent invention.
  4. Spring emergence, the bed's examination: when the book's season turns, the rota counts shoots row by row on the count's stated days, first count at the first honest sign, then fortnightly, and a shoot is counted as the planting card defines it: green growth through the mulch, from a planted position, not a weed at the row's edge and not a hope under the straw. Weeds are the count's standing temptation and the book meets it head-on: anything the counters cannot honestly call garlic is counted as unknown and sketched, never rounded into the crop. The count is entered as a number out of the number planted, row by row, in the same ink whether it delights or disappoints, and the emergence percentage the bed actually grew is written beside the variety's name. The first grade the bed earns, earned by the winter and the planting together, and claimed by neither alone.
  5. Closing the cycle's first year, the bed handed on and the harvest promised in writing: the mulch is drawn back as the book directs, the spring feeding and watering rota, if the site's guidance calls for one, is agreed with named owners for the dry weeks, and the crew writes the bed's closing page together: cloves planted, rejects at planting, emergence by row, the winter's worst weather and what it did, and the one instruction the harvest crew will need most, earned at this bed. The tell-back is said standing at the bed's edge, shoots in rows where the mulch lay: 'In autumn we planted for a harvest we would hand to summer, one clove at a time, pointed up, at a measured depth, in rows the map still matches. Winter did its work without us and the log told the truth about the months we came and the months we did not. Spring graded us in green, row by row, counted shoot by shoot and claimed only where it was seen. The bed keeps the promise through winter, and so, it turns out, did we.' The book, the spare labels and the harvest guidance are stored together where the summer crew will find the whole bed in one place.

Variations

Easier: A single-row version (a first crew or a borrowed bed): one row planted entire by the full discipline, inspection, measured depth, map, blanket, log, counted emergence, because one row planted honestly teaches the whole cycle.

Harder: A container version (no open ground to be had): the deepest pots the site can overwinter outdoors, planted, mulched and logged identically, with the winter log also recording every move the pots are given. A moved pot is a changed winter, and the book says so.

⚠️ Safety

Garden tools set this record's laws and they are stated at the bed before a trowel is lifted: tools are carried point-down at the carrier's side, never carried point-first toward another person, never left point-up in soil or mulch where a kneeling planter will find them with a knee, and the tool count is taken out and back by name. Cloves are a crop, never a snack on planting day: nothing from the bed is tasted by anyone, hands are washed after planting and after every log visit before eating, and any clove that arrives treated or labelled for planting only is handled with gloves and kept entirely out of the kitchen's world. Mulch is forked and laid, never shaken into the air of a still day. A dusty bale is opened by the adult, upwind, and a planter who needs to step away from dust steps away without ceremony. The bed's ground rules are the site's: no work in lightning or on frozen, iron ground, holes are the dibber's job and not a bare hand's, and a cut from any tool, however small, however familiar, is cleaned and covered before work resumes, with the adult told the same hour, because soil and small wounds are a combination the bed treats with respect, not bravado.

🤝 Inclusion notes

A bed divides its authority as fairly as its rows divide the ground: the book-keeper, seated at the bed's edge or at the table the site allows, with the map, the counts and the winter log, holds the bed's memory and reads every count back, which makes the keeper's the casting voice on what the bed has actually done. Planting itself is kneeling, standing and seated work by design: rows can be planted from a kneeling pad, a low stool at the row's side, or standing with a long-handled dibber, and depth is guaranteed by the marked tool, never by a planter's reach or flexibility. Clove inspection is table work at full standing, firmness judged by hand and eye, rejects counted aloud, and a planter may dictate their row's map entry to the keeper verbatim, because no clove's truth depends on one person's handwriting. The winter rota is published a season ahead with named understudies, visits are short and in daylight, and the spring count can be done from photographs the rota takes row by row, counted at the table by counters whose ground is the book rather than the bed; their numbers carry the same ink. Nobody's place in the crew depends on being present in every season. The book, not any person's attendance, is what the harvest crew will read.
