Paper Diya Welcome Row

Big paper diya lamps carried in two hands and set along a floor path, each one lit as it lands — a welcome written in light, one lamp at a time.

Ages
3–3
Group
4–16 kids
Time
10–20 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Make the diyas yourself beforehand — big, stiff, and plain, with a flat spot in the middle where a tea light sits without wobbling when a lamp is set down by small hands; test-carry each one across the room yourself. Say the real thing plainly in your own head before the group hears it: Diwali is the festival of lights, and clay diya lamps are lit and set out to welcome guests and good fortune into a home. Tea lights are battery ones, screwed shut, and they are switched on by adult hands only, at the moment each lamp is placed — the light arriving is the adult's half of the job; the carrying and placing are the kids'.

Materials

  • big paper diya lamps (adult-made: stiff card folded into a shallow boat shape, palm-and-a-half wide, one per kid plus spares)
  • battery tea lights (adult-handled, screwed-shut battery covers, one per lamp)
  • a clear floor path from the door to a rug or reading corner (the welcome route)

Steps

  1. Name the festival in one plain sentence: 'Diwali is a festival of lights. Families light small lamps called diyas and set them where guests come in, to welcome them. We are making a welcome row of lamps along our path.' State the one rule: 'Two hands carry the lamp. Walking feet. You set it down gently where the path is waiting.'
  2. First lamp together, yours: carry a diya in two hands down the path at walking pace, set it down at the path's start with both hands still on it, and switch its light on only once it is placed — then step back with the group and look at one light, alone, doing its job. One lamp is a light; the row is the welcome, and the group should see the difference coming.
  3. Placing round: one carrier at a time, lamp carried in two hands, set down at the next spot along the path — the spots close enough that the lights read as a row, far enough that each placing is its own walk. The carrier walks back empty-handed along the path's edge, never through the lamps. Lit lamps are never picked up again; a lamp that has landed has arrived.
  4. Halfway look: stop the carrying, gather at the door end, and look down the path together — half light, half waiting. Name what is happening in the plainest words: 'The welcome is half built.' Three-year-olds who can SEE the job half done will finish it like builders.
  5. Finish at the last lamp: the final carrier places theirs, the light goes on, and the whole group walks the finished row once, slowly, guest-style, from the door to the rug — arriving the way the lamps were lit to welcome. Then lights off, lamps collected in two hands by their makers, and the row exists in the room's memory at full brightness.

Variations

Easier: A short path of four lamps to the rug only, the adult walking each carry beside the carrier with a hand under the lamp without touching it.

Harder: A second row on the path's other side, placed by the returning carriers — the group judges whether the two rows match lamp for lamp, and a missing lamp is a discovery, not a mistake.

⚠️ Safety

Battery tea lights only — no real flame, no oil, nothing hot exists in this activity at any point. Battery covers stay screwed shut and lights are switched and handled by the adult; a tea light is never a toy and never goes in a mouth, and the lamps are carried, never the lights alone. Carriers walk one at a time along a clear path: a room of three-year-olds all carrying lamps at once is a collision arranged in advance, and stepping over a placed lamp is not a route — the path's edge is.

🤝 Inclusion notes

A lamp's placing counts however it travels: a carrier can carry theirs on a lap tray wheeled along the path's edge, place it seated-to-seated from the row's end, or direct the adult as co-carrier — 'there... down... light it' — with the setting-down and the switching done at their word. The door-end lookout job, calling 'the welcome is growing!' as each light lands, belongs to whoever holds it and runs the whole ceremony.
