Big Float or Sink, First Dip

A tub of water and a shelf of big things: each one is held over the water, dipped once, and reported in the session's only two words. FLOAT or SINK.

Before you begin

Ages
3–3
Group
2–8 kids
Time
10–20 min
Where
Indoors & Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Test every object yourself, alone with the tub, before the session: dip each one and watch what it TRULY does. The sponge that floats for ten seconds and then drowns slowly is this record's treasure and must be scheduled on purpose, not met by accident, and any object whose answer the adult cannot state plainly (half-sunk drifters, things that trap air and flip) leaves the shelf until it behaves or is understood. Set the tub on its towel on a surface a wet sleeve cannot ruin, at a height where small arms reach the water's middle without leaning their whole weight on the rim: a tipped tub is the session's one real accident, and it is prevented here, in the setup, entirely.

Materials

  • a wide shallow tub of water, filled to hand-depth (the dipping sea, big enough that a big object enters without splashing the rim)
  • big test objects, adult-built and adult-tested (things honestly bigger than a palm whose answer is certain: a wooden block, a smooth stone, a big sponge, a cork the size of a small fist, an empty sealed tub with its lid taped, a big leaf, every one dipped by the adult in advance, because this record never schedules a surprise the adult hasn't met)
  • a towel under the tub and a second towel for hands (the shore)

Steps

  1. Bring the group to the tub and say the plain framing: 'Water holds some things up and lets other things go down. That is the whole science, and your hands are going to find out which is which, one thing at a time, dipped once, and reported out loud.' State the laws whole: 'Objects go in SLOWLY, held, and let go only when I say 'let it be'. One dipper at a time. The water stays in the tub, hands come out and dry on the shore towel before the next thing is touched.' Demonstrate the full ceremony on the wooden block yourself: hold it over the water, a breath, lower it in, let it be, watch, and the report in the session's official shape, one word, big and plain: 'FLOAT.'
  2. Dipping round: dippers take turns in known order, one object each, with the same ceremony. Choose from the shelf, hold it over the sea where everyone can see it, the group's one permitted guess said all together BEFORE the dip (guessing is the dip's drumroll and belongs to everyone), then in, let it be, watch together for a full slow count of three, and the dipper's one-word report: FLOAT or SINK. The adult confirms in the same word, same size: 'SINK. The stone went straight down. The water did not hold it.' Reports are about the object, always; a guess that missed is never named again.
  3. Sponge moment, scheduled and savoured: mid-round, the adult brings out the sponge with ceremony. 'This one does something none of the others did. Watch it for longer than you think.', and the group watches it float... and drink... and settle lower, and lower, until it lies on the bottom having changed its answer in front of them. The record's line is ready and true: 'It floated, AND it sank, first one, then the other. Water science has surprises in it even at our size, and watching long enough is how you catch them.' The report for the sponge, officially, is BOTH words, in order. The session's one two-word report, and it is a promotion, not a problem.
  4. Second-dip stretch, for objects the group argues about kindly: an object may be dipped a second time by a different dipper, same ceremony, same watching, because reading a thing twice is what careful people do, and an answer that comes back the same is an answer the room can build on. The adult keeps the tally on fingers, plainly, as it grows: 'Three floats. Two sinks. One both.'. The session's results kept in the open, in numbers a three-year-old can hold.
  5. Finish with the sea emptied properly: objects lifted out one at a time by their dippers, shaken once over the tub, and set on the shore towel in their two honest rows, the floats and the sinks, the sponge in its own place of honour between them, while the tell-back is read off the rows like results: 'The water held the block, the cork, and the little tub. It let the stone go down. And the sponge did both, because we watched long enough. That is the whole report, and every word of it is true.' Hands are dried, the tub is carried by the adult, and the shelf is left dry for next time.

Variations

Easier: A four-object version: block, stone, cork, sponge. Three plain answers and the surprise, inside ten minutes for the newest scientists.

Harder: A leaf-boat version: the big leaf dipped last and ridden by ONE dry leaf-rider (a paper dot). The group's first cargo test, reported in the same two words when the leaf makes its decision.

Safety

The tub is shallow, wide, and settled on its towel before any child reaches it: dippers stand (or sit) beside it with both feet planted, objects are lowered, never dropped or thrown in, a dropped stone in hand-depth water is a splash, a crack risk to the tub, and the end of the watching mood in one move. Every object is palm-sized or bigger, smooth, sealed, and audited before the session; anything that sheds, leaks, cracks, or could invite a mouth leaves the shelf permanently. Water stays in the tub: hands dry on the shore towel between turns, spills are mopped the moment they land by the adult, and wet sleeves are rolled before dipping, not after. No head, face, or hair goes over the water at any time. Looking is done from beside, at the dipper's own height.

Inclusion notes

The dipping works at every station: the tub sits at whatever height puts its middle within the dipper's comfortable reach, an object can be lowered on the adult's steadying hand UNDER the dipper's own, 'your hands decide, mine just carry', and a dipper can direct a whole dip by voice: 'the cork... hold it... LET IT BE... FLOAT.' The report is the session's most open channel by design: one word, spoken, signed, pointed on the adult's two big FLOAT/SINK cards, or stamped out by a happy foot, every form enters the tally identically. The tally-keeper job, holding up the count on fingers for the room after each dip, is the laboratory's official memory and belongs to whoever holds it.
