Growing Degree-Day Ledger

Teens keep a season's heat accounts: daily temperatures become growing degree-days, a budburst prediction is written down, and the plants grade it.

Before you begin

Ages
13–14
Group
4–16 kids
Time
45–60 min
Where
Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Choose the event before you open the ledger, and choose it the way forecasters choose: near enough to check on foot, sharp enough to grade without argument, budburst on a named branch, first open flower on a named shrub, and write its definition on the predictions page now, in the plainest words the crew owns, because a prediction whose event is fuzzy can never be wrong, and a prediction that can never be wrong is worth nothing. Set the ledger's law at setup and write it on the first page: temperatures are entered daily at the stated hour from the stated source, a missed day is entered as missed and estimated never, and predictions are written in ink, dated, and signed. A graded miss in ink teaches more than a pencilled success rewritten after the fact. Work one example day together before the season starts: high, low, mean, minus the base, floored at zero. The arithmetic is small and the crew does it by hand all season, because a spreadsheet that computes silently teaches nobody where degree-days come from. Agree the checking round now: who walks to the named plant on which days, and what they report back, seen, not seen, or nearly, in the ledger's own three words.

Materials

  • one max-min thermometer, or the site's agreed weather source read at the same hour every day (the ledger's whole honesty is sameness: same source, same hour, same handwriting in the temperature columns. A prediction built on mixed sources is a rumour with arithmetic on it)
  • the ledger itself, a hardback book ruled before the season starts: date, high, low, mean, the day's degree-days, the running total, and a predictions page whose entries are dated and never altered, only graded
  • one named event to predict, chosen for being observable from the site's own ground (a named tree's budburst, a bed's first flower, the lilac by the gate. Something the crew can check with their own eyes on a stated round, not a photograph sent by somebody else)
  • the base-temperature card for the chosen plant, agreed with the adults from a trusted gardening source and copied onto the ledger's first page word for word (plants count heat above their own base, and the base is a fact about the plant, not a number the crew negotiates)

Steps

  1. The framing, at the ledger's table, the named plant in view if the ground allows: 'A plant keeps its calendar in heat, not in dates. The shrub by the gate does not know what April is. It knows how much warmth has arrived since winter. It adds the days up in its own arithmetic, and when the sum crosses its number, it opens. Farmers and growers have kept that arithmetic for centuries, and it has a name: growing degree-days. Every day gives the plant a payment, the day's mean temperature, minus the base below which this plant counts nothing, and the payments accumulate in this ledger, in our handwriting, at the same hour, from the same source, all season. When our running total approaches the sum the references say this event usually needs, this crew will do the brave thing in ink: predict the date. Then the plant grades us. Not kindly, not cruelly, exactly. A crew that has predicted a budburst and been graded by the bush itself has learned the difference between a forecast and a feeling, and that difference is the whole of this record.' The base card is read aloud, the first day's temperatures are entered together, and the ledger's law is signed by the crew.
  2. The daily entries, the ledger's long quiet discipline: each day's high and low are entered at the stated hour by the rota's named keeper, the mean and the day's degree-days worked in the margin where the arithmetic can be checked by any reader, and the running total carried forward in the same hand. The rota is published a fortnight ahead with named understudies, because a ledger with a silent fortnight in it is a forecast with a hole where its evidence should be, and where a day IS missed, the entry says MISSED in the keeper's hand, the degree-days for that day are left blank, and the running total carries a mark every reader can see, so the prediction that follows knows the size of its own uncertainty. Once a week the crew audits the ledger together: totals re-added from the daily column, discrepancies found and entered as corrections with their dates, never erased, because a ledger that hides its corrections is asking to be believed, and this one is asking to be checked.
  3. The prediction, entered in ink before the event, the record's summit: when the running total enters the range the references give for the event, the crew convenes at the table with the ledger, the base card and the season so far, the warm spells, the cold snap that paid nothing for a week, the missed days marked in the margin. Each member states a predicted date and the reasoning in one sentence, the total's pace, the forecast's next week, the plant's own look on the last round, and the crew's prediction is agreed, written on the predictions page, dated and signed by every member, including anyone whose own date differed, because a crew prediction is a position taken together. From that day the checking round walks daily: seen, not seen, or nearly, entered in the ledger's three words with the walker's initials, and nobody, whatever the total says, announces the event from a window, a photograph or a hope. The plant is checked at the plant.
  4. The grading, done by the plant and recorded without flinching: on the day the round reports SEEN, the ledger is opened at the table and the prediction is graded in the open, days early, days late, or on the date, and the grade is written beside the prediction in the same ink as everything else in the book, with the running total the plant actually used, computed from the ledger's own columns. Then the crew does the analysis this record exists for: where did the arithmetic mislead, a warm spell the references' range did not expect, a base temperature that fits this plant in this yard imperfectly, missed days whose blank the crew must now price honestly, and each finding is entered as a finding, in words, because next season's crew inherits this book, and a graded prediction with its reasoning is worth ten ungraded successes. The tell-back for the grading day is said at the plant itself: 'We said the heat would open this by a date we wrote in ink. The plant has now told us what its calendar actually said. We were out by the number in the book, and the book shows our working, our missed days, our warm spell and our grade, all in the same hand. That is what a forecast is: not being right, but being checkable.'
  5. Closing the season, the ledger handed on as the site's heat memory: the crew writes the season's closing page together, the event, the predicted date, the actual date, the grade, the total the plant used, the warmest and coldest weeks, and the one instruction next season's crew will need most, earned at this table. Where the site keeps more than one season's books, this ledger is shelved with them in date order, because a run of graded predictions across years is the beginning of the site's own local knowledge. The references give ranges for the species, but only this ground's ledgers can say what the sum is HERE, and that sentence, earned over seasons, is the record's real harvest. The closing tell-back is said over the shut book: 'In spring we opened a ledger and promised a plant we would count its heat honestly, same hour, same source, missed days marked, predictions in ink. The plant kept its side: it opened when its sum arrived. We kept ours: the book can be checked by anyone, and our grade is in it unedited. A plant keeps its calendar in heat, not in dates, and now, on this ground, so do we.' The thermometer, the base card and the ledger are stored together where the next season's crew will find the whole watch in one place.

Variations

Easier: A single-plant version (a small crew or a late start): one named shrub, one prediction, the full discipline, daily entries, ink, the daily round, the grade, because one prediction kept honestly teaches the whole arithmetic.

Harder: A two-event version (a returning crew): a second named event on a different plant with its own base card, both predicted from the same ledger, and the closing page compares the two grades, the crew learns which plant its ground understands better, and says so.

Safety

The ledger's work is arithmetic and walking, and its laws are a walker's laws: the checking round goes in daylight, by the site's agreed paths, in the crew's stated pairs, and nobody crosses fences, walls or a neighbour's boundary to reach a plant whose owner has not agreed the crew may visit it, a plant that cannot be visited lawfully is not this record's plant, however perfect its budburst. Thermometers are read where they hang; a glass thermometer is handled by the adult alone if the site still keeps one, and a broken one ends the day's entries until the adults have dealt with it, the ledger records the gap honestly rather than anyone handling glass and its contents bare-handed. Sun and season are respected as the ledger respects everything: the round is short, hats and water follow the site's ordinary hot-day rules, and a round postponed by lightning or dangerous heat is entered as postponed, in ink, without apology. The plant is not checking whether the crew is brave. Predictions are the record's sport and stay kind: a member whose date was furthest out is never the butt of the grading day, the grade belongs to the crew entire, and the book's language for error is arithmetic, early, late, by how much, never a name.

Inclusion notes

A ledger is a table art, and its honours sit at the table: the keeper of the day, reader of the thermometer or the source, worker of the mean, carrier of the running total, holds the season's evidence in their hand, and the auditor's role at the weekly check, re-adding the columns and hunting the discrepancies, is an office of pure attention that any member can hold at full standing from a chair. The checking round divides its labour honestly: walkers report in the ledger's three words, and a member whose ground is the table rather than the path can hold the round's other end, receiving the report, entering it, initialling it beside the walker's name, with the entry carrying exactly the same weight, because the ledger grades the report's honesty, not the reporter's legs. Arithmetic is done aloud and in the margin by whoever works it, checked by a second hand as a matter of the book's law rather than anyone's ability, and the prediction meeting hears every member's date stated in whatever register they command, speech, writing, a finger on the calendar, each entered in the book in the same ink. Nobody's season depends on never missing a day: the rota has understudies by design, a missed day is a mark in a margin, and the crew that keeps the book is the crew whose names are in it, all of them.
