New Game Playtest League

Teams invent a movement game, write its rules, watch strangers break them, revise, and republish — the league rewards the clearest game, not the loudest.

Ages
13–14
Group
6–30 kids
Time
45–60 min
Where
Indoors & Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Set the league's constitution before any team invents anything, and set it where everyone can read it all season: games are played on feet in the room's real space, from the shared shelf's kit; every game must be non-elimination by structure — nobody sits out, nobody is out, a player who is beaten by a moment re-enters by the game's own rule — because a league that eliminates has confused drama with design; scoring must be countable by a spectator; and a game is not entered until its rules fit on one card and a stranger has read them. The adult's screen is defined in the same pass and published with the constitution: no contact beyond a tag's touch, no projectiles at heads, no game whose speed exceeds the room's stopping distance, nothing climbed, nothing thrown that the shelf calls hard. Lay out the season's shape now — invention round, stranger tests, revision, the league fixtures — with dates, because a league is a promise of rematches, and teams design differently when they know the strangers are coming back.

Materials

  • the league's equipment shelf (one shared, inventoried set every team designs from — soft balls, cones, bibs, ropes, hoops: a game that needs kit the shelf does not hold is a poem, not an entry)
  • rules cards and markers, one card per game (the game's whole law on one card: players, space, start, scoring, the end — a game whose rules need a second card is not finished being invented)
  • the playtest log (one page per game per test: who tested, what broke, what the designers changed — the league's memory and its real league table's evidence)
  • the adult's safety screen, applied to every entry before its first test (the league's one non-negotiable gate, described in the framing and never waived for a clever game)

Steps

  1. The framing, shelf open, cards blank: 'A rule that only works when everyone is being nice is not a rule yet. This league does not reward the game that sounds best in its inventors' mouths. It rewards the game that survives strangers: people who did not hear the explanation, who read one card, who play to win, find the loopholes, stand exactly where the designer did not imagine, and ask the question the card cannot answer. Every team invents a game today. Then the strangers arrive — each other's teams, reading only the card — and the game gets broken in the open, and that breakage is the league's gift, not its embarrassment. Designers watch their own tests in silence, log in hand, and may not explain, plead or demonstrate: the card speaks, or the card is rewritten. At the season's end the league table ranks the games by how they played for strangers — clarity first, because a game nobody can start is a game nobody can love.' The constitution is read aloud with the framing, the screen's rules named entire, and the silence rule for designers demonstrated once, briefly, to general discomfort and complete understanding.
  2. Invention round: teams build from the shelf and the room, testing fragments on themselves as they go — a scoring idea tried for two minutes, a boundary moved, a start re-drawn — and the card is drafted last, from what the fragments actually did, not first from what the team imagined at the table. The card's five fields are the round's discipline: players (how many, arranged how), space (marked how, out is where), start (the exact words or signal), scoring (countable, public, kept by whom), and the end (when it stops and what is true then). A field the team cannot fill is a hole the strangers will find at full speed, and the round's coaches — the adult and the league's own first questions — push on exactly those fields: 'What happens when both reach it at once?' is the league's favourite sentence, and a card that has survived it three times is nearly a game. Entries close when every card passes the screen and the one-card law; a game held back by the screen gets its reason in writing on its log page, and its team revises and re-enters, because the gate is a design tool, not a bin.
  3. Stranger tests, the league's examination hall: each game is set up by its designers from its card, then handed over — the designers retire to the log table, silent, while a stranger team reads the card aloud, asks the card its questions, and plays. The log page fills with the test's true events in the order they happen: where the strangers stalled, what they argued about and what the card said (or failed to say) about it, every loophole walked through at full competitive honesty, every moment the game caught fire and played itself — those go in too, in the same ink, because a revision that only fixes faults can break the magic it did not notice. The designers' silence is total and it is the hardest rule in the building; a designer who calls out a correction has marked their own card as failed at that exact line, and the log says so, kindly and permanently, because the lesson is the league's whole point: the card must carry the game when its inventors are not in the room — and someday its inventors will not be in the room.
  4. Revision and republication: teams take their logs home to the card and rebuild — every breakage answered by a rule, a boundary, a sentence; every patch read aloud against the log to check it mends the actual hole and not a neighbouring one; the card rewritten clean at one card still, because a game that needs its patches stapled on has been revised in the wrong tense. Revised games are re-tested by different strangers — the league never lets the same strangers certify a fix they inspired — and the log gains its second page: what survived, what broke anew (revisions breed fresh loopholes the way fresh paint shows fresh cracks, and the log expects them), and the designers' verdict on their own game, entered signed: fit for the fixtures, or back for one more round, with the evidence cited either way. A team that withdraws its game at this point, on its own log's evidence, is recorded in the league's honours with the same prominence as a fixture winner — knowing a game is not ready, and proving it, is designing.
  5. League fixtures and the table: the surviving games are played in fixtures across the remaining sessions — every team playing every game, hosts rotating, scores kept by the hosts on the log's fixture page — and the league table is published in its true shape, which is not a ladder of players but of games: each game ranked by the strangers' marks for clarity (could we start from the card?), fairness (did the rules treat both sides alike when pressed?), and fire (did it play itself at its best moments?) — with the designers' revisions between tests counted as the league's respect, not its suspicion. The closing tell-back is said with the cards and logs on the table between the teams: 'Every game here was broken by strangers and mended in writing, some of them twice. That is not a failed afternoon of games — that is the entire history of every game anybody has ever loved, compressed into a season and done honestly. A rule that only works when everyone is being nice is not a rule yet; yours work when everyone is trying to win. Take the cards. The games are yours now, and they will outlive this room, because strangers can start them without you.' The cards and logs are filed as the league's book; next season's teams inherit them as the shelf's senior games, credited to their inventors by name.

Variations

Easier: A one-afternoon version: two teams, two games, one stranger test and one revision each — the whole league in miniature, with the silence rule and the one-card law kept entire, because they are the league.

Harder: A festival version (a whole programme day): visiting groups are the strangers, fixtures run all day on a published card, and the league table is announced at the day's end — the games are marked by people who will never meet their inventors, which is the purest test the league can buy.

⚠️ Safety

The adult's screen is the league's first law and it gates every entry before its first stranger test, without exception or appeal at the table: no contact beyond a tag's flat touch, no projectiles aimed at heads, nothing climbed or swung from, no game whose playing speed outruns the room's stopping distance, and every game non-elimination by structure — a beaten player re-enters by the game's own rule, because a test that benches its strangers teaches designers nothing about flow. The screen is re-applied after every revision, since a patch can smuggle in what the original never carried, and any moment in any test that risks a body stops that game instantly on the adult's word, with the stoppage and its reason entered on the log as design evidence, never as a team's disgrace. Space is the screen's silent partner: fixtures are set with run-off margins the constitution names, spectators stand behind the line the hosts mark, and the shelf's hard kit stays shelved for this league regardless of what a game requests. Water and rest are scheduled between fixtures by the hosts; enthusiasm is not a hydration plan.

🤝 Inclusion notes

Invention is a table with many seats, and this league's honours run through all of them: the card's five fields are written work, the log is kept at the test's edge by the designers' own recorders — a seated job with the league's whole memory in its hands — and the revision round, where games are actually won, is talk and text at the table, where a designer who never ran a fixture can be the mind that finds the patch. Play itself is built for the room: the constitution's non-elimination law means every player stays in every game, the shelf and the space are the same for all inventors, and a game's easier and harder doors — most teams design them once the strangers have shown them where the game pinches — are praised in the table's marks when they appear. Stranger tests are read aloud from the card as a rule, signals for starts are agreed per game in sight as well as sound, and fixture hosting rotates through every member who wants the whistle, the slate or the boundary line. A withdrawn game, honestly logged, stands in the honours beside the winners — the league's way of saying that judgement, in any body, is the sport.
