Microclimate Map Survey

Teens read temperature, shade and wind at a grid of stations in one place at one hour, and draw the map of its many small climates.

Ages 13–14 · 45–60 min · 4 materials

Ages
13–14
Group
6–18 kids
Time
45–60 min
Where
Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Walk the ground before you promise the map anything, and choose the stations the way the record demands: the place's honest contrasts, the wall that hoards the afternoon sun, the dip where cold air settles, the tree's deep shade, the open middle that gets everything the sky sends. Number the stations on the base sketch and pace the route each team will walk, because the survey's central discipline is time: every station must be read inside the same short window, or the map compares different hours and calls them different places. Agree the method card now and write it on the map's margin as law, thermometer height, two minutes' settle, shade-the-sensor, wind judged by the ribbon's behaviour on the agreed scale, shade called by the shadow's own edge. Choose the survey hour for contrast, not convenience, a still, bright hour when walls and shade are doing their different work, and accept the record's weather law in advance: a survey rained off is postponed entire, never half-read, because half a map is a whole error. Assign teams their stations so every team reads a mix of sun and shade; a team that only reads the sunny stations will draw the map it expects, and this survey exists to defeat expectations, including its own.

Materials

  • a grid of stations agreed and marked before the survey hour (eight to twelve stations across one real place, the yard, the garden, the school ground: against the wall, under the tree, on the open stones, in the dip, each numbered on a base sketch the surveyor carries, because a reading without a place is a number looking for its map)
  • thermometers enough for the teams, of one kind, read the same way (the survey's honesty is simultaneity and sameness: all stations read inside the same ten minutes, every thermometer shaded from direct sun at the same height, and the method card, height, shade, wait time, written on the map's margin before the first reading)
  • the wind and shade kit (a ribbon wand or a handful of grass seed for wind, a simple scale agreed at setup, still, stirring, steady, and the shade call made by the same rule at every station: full sun, edge, or deep shade, judged by the shadow map the hour actually casts)
  • the map sheet itself. A large sheet with the place's fixed furniture drawn in advance (walls, trees, paths, water) by the survey's cartographer, left with a blank key for the temperature bands the day will earn, because the map is drawn from the readings, never decorated into them

Steps

  1. The framing, at the map table, the base sketch in the middle and the place itself in view: 'One yard holds many climates. Stand against that wall at three o'clock and you are in a warmer country than the dip by the hedge, same hour, same sky, different weather. Gardeners know it, lizards know it, and by the end of this hour this crew will have it on paper: a map of the small climates inside one place, read at a grid of stations inside one ten-minute window, every thermometer used the same way, every call made by the same rules. The map is the argument. We are not drawing what we believe. The wall is not allowed to be warm on our map because we feel warm looking at it. It will be warm if its station's number says so, read in shade, at the agreed height, inside the window. And where the numbers surprise us, a cold pocket where we promised warmth, a sheltered corner warmer than the open stones, the surprise goes on the map entire, because a survey that only confirms its crew is a portrait, not a survey.' Stations are issued, the method card is read aloud once, and the teams walk their routes at rehearsal pace before the window opens.
  2. The window, the survey's ten honest minutes: at the keeper's call the teams move to their first stations and the settling begins, thermometers placed by the method card, the two minutes counted without fidgeting, because a sensor lifted early reads the hand that held it, not the place it stands in. Each station is read entire before the team moves: temperature to the agreed precision, wind on the ribbon scale, shade by the shadow's edge, and anything the method card did not predict, the tap dripping at station four, the ants boiling out of station seven's stones, entered in the station's margin, because a map's margins are where its next questions live. Readings are called back to the map table by runner or by the team's return at the window's close, and the keeper enters each number on the station's cell in the hearing of the team that took it. Entered as read, never rounded toward the map anyone expected, and a reading a team distrusts is re-taken inside the window by the method, or marked as doubted in the margin, in the open.
  3. The drawing, where numbers become a country: with all stations in, the crew bands the temperatures together at the table, the day's own range divided into the key's bands by the numbers the window actually returned, not by a key copied from a book, and the cartographer colours or hatches each station's ground by its band, the wind calls drawn as arrows of the agreed weight, the shade calls as the shadow's true outline at the survey hour. Then the map is read as an argument, aloud, station by station: where is this place warmest at this hour, and what stands there, a wall, a stone, a south face? Where is it coldest, and what makes it so. The dip, the hedge's shade, the wind's corridor between the buildings? Every claim in the reading must point at a station's numbers; a claim that cannot is struck from the map's conclusions and entered, kindly, in the margin as a belief the survey did not test. The finished map is dated, houred, and weather-noted on its face, because a microclimate map without its hour is a rumour about a place.
  4. The planting test, the map put to work before it is filed: the crew takes the finished map back onto the ground with one practical question the site actually has, where should the tender seedlings harden off, where does the frost settle first, where would a bench be warmest for the winter readers or a water butt slowest to freeze, and the map's answer is walked to and checked at the stations it names, in the open, with the thermometers if the hour still holds. A map that sends its crew to the wrong corner is graded as honestly as any forecast: the stations are re-read, the method re-examined, and the correction entered on the map's margin with its date, because this survey's maps are working documents, not wall art. Where the site's gardeners or groundskeepers will use the map, the crew presents it standing at the place itself, conclusions first, numbers behind every conclusion, surprises named with the same pride as confirmations, and the map's limits are stated by its own makers: one hour, one day's weather, one season, and the invitation to survey again when any of the three has changed.
  5. Closing the survey, the map filed as the place's first climate record: the crew writes the survey's closing note on the map's back. The window, the method card's terms, the warmest and coldest stations with their numbers, the surprise the map is proudest of, and the one instruction the next survey crew will need most, earned on this ground. The tell-back is said over the finished map, the real place visible beyond it: 'An hour ago this was one yard with one weather. It is twelve stations now, and at least three countries: the wall's warm shelf, the open stones' honest middle, and the dip where the cold collects like water. We read them in the same ten minutes, by the same rules, and we drew what the numbers said, including the corner that refused to be what we expected. One yard holds many climates. This map is how we know, and anyone who doubts it can take the thermometers and check our stations, which is exactly what it is for.' The map, the station sketch, the method card and the raw station sheets are filed together where the next survey, another hour, another season. Will find the whole watch in one place, ready to be argued with by evidence.

Variations

Easier: A four-station version (a small crew or a short hour): the place's four strongest contrasts read with the full discipline, same window, same method, the map drawn and read entire, because four honest stations defeat one vague impression of a whole yard.

Harder: A two-season version (a returning crew): the identical stations, method and hour surveyed again in the opposite season, the two maps pinned together, and every station's change entered as a finding. The place's climates have biographies, and this crew has read two chapters.

⚠️ Safety

The survey's ground is walked before it is surveyed, and its laws are the site's own walking laws: stations are placed only where the crew may lawfully and safely stand, no station inside a works area, a car park's moving lanes, a neighbour's boundary or a patch of ground whose hazard the adults have not cleared, and a station that cannot be stood at safely is moved and the move entered on the sketch, never improvised at the window. Thermometers are carried point-safe and read at arm's length; a glass thermometer, where the site still keeps one, is the adult's instrument alone, and a breakage ends that team's window. The station is marked unread and the reason entered, because the method outranks the completeness of any single map. Sun is the survey's subject and its hazard at once: the window is short by design, hats and water follow the site's hot-day rules, teams rotate through shade between stations where the route allows, and the keeper may call the window closed early for heat, lightning or a turn in the weather. A shortened window is entered on the map's face as its true condition, never silently completed from memory. Ribbons, sketches and loose sheets are kept out of water butts, drains and planting beds, and every marker the crew sets out is counted back in by name at the close.

🤝 Inclusion notes

The survey staffs every kind of attention at full standing: the keeper at the map table, caller of the window, receiver of the readings, enterer of every number in the hearing of its team, runs the survey's clock and its truth from the place's most comfortable seat, and the cartographer's office, drawing bands and arrows from the returned numbers, is table work whose decisions shape the whole map. Stations are assigned by the ground each member can honestly work: the route is planned step-free where the place allows, a station on rough ground can be read by a partnered team whose reader stands where the footing is sure and calls the reading to a recorder at the path. The number carries the same ink either way, because the method governs the reading, not the reader's route. Readings are called aloud and entered twice-checked as the survey's law, judgements of wind and shade are made by the agreed scales rather than by anyone's eyesight alone, and the map-reading at the table hears every team's margin notes in whatever register they were kept, spoken, written, or pointed out on the sketch, each conclusion tested against the same stations. Nobody's survey depends on covering the whole grid: the grid is covered by the crew, and the crew is everybody in it.
