Cup Anemometer Wind Lab

Build the weather station's spinning cups, count the turns, and turn a summer week of breezes into numbers you can argue with.

Before you begin

Ages
9–13
Group
3–24 kids
Time
40–60 min
Where
Indoors & Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Build one instrument yourself before the session and spin it outdoors; a cup anemometer that wobbles or stalls teaches the lab's first lesson early, which is that axle friction is the enemy and the bead spacer is the fix. Agree the counting law now, in writing on the log's cover: count only the coloured cup passing one fixed point, count for exactly thirty seconds, count in pairs (one watches, one counts aloud), and log the place and the hour with every reading, because wind numbers without a place and an hour are rumours. Choose the lab's three stations (an open field corner, a sheltered wall, a doorway or gap between buildings) and mark them on a sketch map in the log.

Materials

  • four small paper or plastic cups per instrument, plus one coloured with a marker (the counting cup)
  • two stiff straws or thin dowels per instrument, crossed and taped at the centre
  • a push pin or nail, a pencil with an eraser top, and a bead, per instrument (the axle: pin through the straw cross, into the eraser, bead as spacer)
  • a stopwatch or phone timer, and a wind log: date, time, place, turns in 30 seconds, and what the air felt like
  • the Beaufort scale card: the class-agreed version, copied from a trusted weather source onto the log's cover (calm under 1, up to storm)

Steps

  1. Build: cups stapled or taped to the four straw ends, all openings facing the same way around the cross. That sameness is the entire invention: wind pushes the hollow side harder than the round side, so the cross turns, and the turning can be counted. Spin each finished instrument by blowing gently; fix wobbles before going out.
  2. Calibration by feel: at station one, take a reading and immediately write what the air did to your clothes, hair and the nearest leaves or grass. Then read the Beaufort card and find the row that matches what you felt. The instrument gives a number; the scale gives the number a meaning; the body checks both.
  3. Station rounds: every crew reads all three stations inside one fifteen-minute window, so the comparison is honest. Log turns per thirty seconds at each station, twice, and keep the lower-friction reading (the higher count) with a note if the two disagreed wildly.
  4. The map: crews transfer their numbers onto the sketch map and the room argues the pattern. Where was the wind fastest, and why there? Gaps between buildings squeeze air; open corners catch it; walls make eddies. A good lab ends with a claim the map supports and a claim it refuses to support.
  5. The week: the log stays open for five days of readings at the agreed hour. Crews predict tomorrow's ranking of the three stations each day, in writing, before reading. The week grades the predictions, and the final session names the room's most reliable station and its most surprising one.
  6. Close by retiring the instruments properly: repair one, file the log with the map, and write the lab's one-sentence finding on the cover (for example: the doorway beat the field four days out of five). Findings written on covers get read next summer.

Variations

Easier: Build from a pre-made cross, count for a full minute (easier arithmetic), and read one station only, calibrating by feel and the Beaufort card.

Harder: Convert counts to estimated wind speeds with a researched conversion the crew derives themselves: time a known walk, compare with a trusted weather reading for the same hour, and build the lab's own counts-to-speed table, errors and all.

Safety

Pins and nails are handled at the build table only, points down in a cork when idle; instruments are carried by the pencil shaft, never swung. Outdoor rounds follow the site's weather rules: no lab in thunder weather, ever.

Source: Wikipedia, en.wikipedia.org

Why it works

The cup anemometer has measured wind since 1846 because it turns an invisible force into a countable rotation; calibrating counts against felt experience and a standard scale teaches what measurement is for.

Inclusion notes

Counting, timing, logging and mapping are full lab roles for anyone who does not build; the coloured-cup counting method works by watching, listening for a partner's count, or tallying on a clicker.
