First Rounds: Singing in Canon

One song, many starting lines: the group learns to sing the same tune at different times and hears it turn into harmony.

Before you begin

Ages
7–11
Group
8–30 kids
Time
20–30 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Arrange the circle and split it into three or four arcs before anyone sings; each arc is one entry group and will start the song at a different time. Choose the round with the group, not for them: offer two and let them vote, because a round only locks in when every singer owns the tune. Agree the entry signal now (the leader holds up one finger per group, in order) and the stopping rule (the leader shows a flat palm; everyone finishes the line they are on and stops together). Set the room's one law: nobody sings louder to fix a wobble; you fix a round by listening harder, not by shouting.

Materials

  • no materials at all: voices only
  • a set of three short rounds the leader knows cold (public-domain staples such as Row, Row, Row Your Boat and Frere Jacques work; pick ones your group already half-knows)
  • floor markers or chairs in a circle, one spot per singer, so each entry group can see its own corner of the room

Steps

  1. Sing the chosen round all together, in unison, twice through, until the tune sits in the room without the leader carrying it. Slow is fine. Boring is fine. This is the foundation pour and it cannot be rushed.
  2. Teach the entry map with hands before voices: the leader points to arc one, which hums the first line; points to arc two, which hums the first line while arc one hums the second. Walk the whole map humming, so the shape is understood before a single word is sung.
  3. First true entry: arc one sings line one alone. On the leader's signal, arc two enters at line one while arc one moves to line two. Let it wobble; wobbling is the sound of a group learning to hold its own line while another line happens beside it.
  4. Add the third arc on the next run. When all arcs are in, the room is singing one song in three places at once. Name what just happened, plainly: the same melody, started at different times, fitting together. That is a round, and musicians have built them this way for centuries.
  5. Run the full round three times through and end on the flat-palm signal. Ask each arc to describe what it heard from the arcs beside it; the answers teach more than another run would.
  6. Swap the arcs' entry order and run it again. The group that entered last now leads, and everyone learns the round is a structure, not a ladder: there is no best seat in a canon.
  7. Close by teaching the room one new round to take away, the simplest the leader knows, sung once in unison and once in two parts. The takeaway is the point: a round needs no instruments, no copies and no kit, only people who can hold a line.

Variations

Easier: Two arcs only, and a four-line round the group already knows from younger years; enter on a drum tap instead of a finger signal if watching and singing at once is too much.

Harder: Four arcs, a round in a minor key, and a student conductor: the conductor owns the entries, the tempo and the cut-off, and the room reviews the conducting the way it reviewed its own singing.

Safety

Nobody sings alone at any point; every voice is always inside a group of voices. A singer who goes quiet mid-round simply rejoins at the next line: the structure holds them.

Source: Wikipedia, en.wikipedia.org

Why it works

Part-singing is the oldest documented form of group harmony teaching: a round asks for one melody learned once, then layers it against itself, so the harmony is built into the material rather than added by arrangement.

Inclusion notes

Singers who do not use their voice can keep an arc's entry on a shaker or a tapped knee pattern that follows the same entry map; the canon works with any steady sound source. Keep arcs small enough that no singer is ever the only voice on a line.
