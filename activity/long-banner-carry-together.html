Long Banner Carry-Together

A long paper banner and a hand every few steps along its edge: the whole crew walks it across the room flat, or it does not arrive at all.

Before you begin

Ages
3–3
Group
6–18 kids
Time
10–20 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Make the banner before the group gathers. Long enough to need at least six hands, light enough that a three-year-old's grip is real, and already decorated or carrying one big word, so what is being carried visibly matters. Walk the route yourself carrying one edge: it must be straight, flat and boring, because the carrying is the event and the route should contribute nothing. Mark the hanging spot at the far end at adult height; the arrival has a destination the crew can see.

Materials

  • one long banner (a roll of paper or joined sheets, adult-made, with a welcome word or the group's own marks on it)
  • a clear straight route across the room
  • tape or clips at the far wall (adult's, for hanging)

Steps

  1. Show the banner laid flat on the floor and say the plain truth of it: 'This banner is too long for two hands. It needs a hand every little while along its edge. That is us. If we all walk together, it stays flat. If one of us hurries, it folds and tears.' State the one rule: 'Two hands or one hand on the edge, walking feet, and we start and stop on the call.'
  2. Crewing round: the group spreads along one side, and the bigger kids of the crew along both sides if numbers allow, each carrier finding their own hand-span of edge and gripping it gently, paper held, not pulled. You call the lift: 'Hands on... lift', and the banner comes off the floor as one thing, which the group will feel happen in their own hands.
  3. The carry: walking feet, your pace set at the front, the banner travelling flat across the room. Call one stop halfway, 'Stop', and let the crew feel the banner hang still and level between them: holding a thing still together is harder than moving it together, and three-year-olds can feel that fact without a word of explanation.
  4. Arrive: at the far wall the crew holds the banner up and steady, arms at whatever height their grip reaches, while you tape or clip the top edge. Their holding IS the hanging; a banner that goes up while its crew holds it has been installed by the crew, and everyone present knows it.
  5. Finish with the step-back: whole crew behind you, looking at the banner on the wall, flat because they walked it flat. Name the crew's work in one plain sentence, 'Every hand on that edge is why it is up there whole', then the banner stays up for the day, and carriers will point their hand-span out to anyone who visits.

Variations

Easier: A shorter banner and a crew of four, the adult carrying the middle span where the sag lives. The crew still feels the banner need every hand it has.

Harder: A corner to turn: the route bends once around a table, and the crew discovers the outside of the bend must walk further than the inside, called, practised, and carried at a shuffle.

Safety

Walking only, and the pace is the slowest carrier's. A banner carried at a run tears, and the tear is the lesson nobody needs twice. Grips are gentle and edges are never yanked from another carrier's hands; a dropped edge is re-gripped on the stop call, not grabbed at mid-stride. The hanging is the adult's job at adult height: carriers hold, the adult tapes, and nobody climbs to reach the wall.

Source: Wikipedia: Banner

Inclusion notes

The banner visits every carrier: a seated carrier's hand-span can be at the route's halfway station, where the banner's edge is carried TO their hands for the stop-and-hold. The halfway hold the carry genuinely needs a crew member stationed to do. A carrier can also walk their span wheeling with one hand on the edge, the paper light enough that the grip is the whole job, and the banner arrives exactly as flat.
