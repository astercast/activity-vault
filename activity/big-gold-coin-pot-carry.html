Big Gold Coin Pot Carry

Palm-sized pretend gold coins carried in two hands, one at a time, and dropped into the big pot with a clink — the whole group's carrying, collected.

Ages
3–3
Group
4–16 kids
Time
10–20 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Make the coins yourself beforehand — big, stiff, and honestly pretend: gold card, palm-and-a-half wide, no foil small enough to peel. Say the framing plainly in your own head before the group hears it: St. Patrick's Day is a real celebration, and the pot of gold at the rainbow's end is a pretend story people tell with it — the record keeps those two things separate and so does the session. Test the pot's clink yourself: the coin landing must be HEARD, because the sound is the receipt each carrier is carrying toward.

Materials

  • big pretend gold coins (adult-made: stiff gold card discs, wider than a kid's palm, edges smooth, one per kid plus spares)
  • one big pot (a sturdy bucket or plastic cauldron) at the path's end
  • a short clear carrying path from the coin basket to the pot

Steps

  1. Name the day and the story in two plain sentences: 'Today is for St. Patrick's Day. People tell a pretend story about a pot of gold at the end of a rainbow — it is a story, not a real thing, and stories are for playing. We are playing it big.' State the one rule: 'Two hands carry the coin. Walking feet. The coin goes in the pot, and only in the pot.'
  2. First coin together, yours: carry a coin in two hands down the path at walking pace, hold it over the pot, and let it drop — CLINK — while the group listens. Say what just happened in the smallest words: 'One coin, carried all the way, in the pot.' Then look in together: one coin looks lonely in a big pot, which is exactly the problem this group is about to solve together.
  3. Carrying round: one carrier at a time, coin in two hands, walking feet, the drop done by the carrier's own hands over the pot. Carriers walk back empty-handed along the path's edge. A dropped coin on the way is picked up by its own carrier and the carry simply continues — the path forgives, the pot waits, and nobody's carry is ever re-done by anyone else.
  4. Halfway listen: stop the carrying, gather round the pot, and have the last carrier drop a coin in while everyone stays quiet for the clink and the rattle of the coins already there. Name the change out loud: 'The pot sounds different already — fuller.' Three-year-olds who can HEAR the job half done will finish it like savers.
  5. Finish when every coin is in: the final carrier drops theirs, the group counts the pot's coins by lifting them out one at a time back into the basket — the count is the second game hiding inside the first — and the tell-back is plain: 'Every coin in this pot got here in two hands, at walking pace, one carrier at a time.' Coins away, pot away, story closed as a story.

Variations

Easier: A four-coin pot only and a path of six steps, the adult walking each carry beside the carrier with a hand hovering under the coin without touching it.

Harder: Two pots and a sorted carry: gold coins to the big pot, plain card coins to the small one — carriers must read their coin before they set out and walk to the right pot.

⚠️ Safety

Coins are big stiff card, far too wide for a mouth and with nothing peelable or detachable on them — a coin that gets chewed or starts to delaminate retires on the spot. The pot is sturdy and stays where the adult put it; nobody climbs in, sits in, or reaches deep inside it while others are dropping — drops happen over the rim, at arm's length, one carrier at a time. Carrying is walking, always: a running carrier holding a coin at face height with both hands has no hand free to fall on, and the rule exists for exactly that reason.

🤝 Inclusion notes

A coin's carry counts however it travels: on a lap tray wheeled along the path's edge, held against a chest in two hands from a seated carry-position at the pot's side, or directed — 'carry it... drop it NOW' — while the adult's hands do exactly what the director's words say. The pot-side listener job, calling 'CLINK — one more in!' at every landing, belongs to whoever holds it and is the pot's official scorekeeper.
