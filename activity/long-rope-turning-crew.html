Long Rope Turning Crew

Two turners, one long rope, a whole crew jumping in turn: turning is the skilled job, entries are timed to a moving rope, and nobody jumps alone.

Ages
13–14
Group
6–24 kids
Time
30–50 min
Where
Indoors & Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Choose the ground before you choose the figures, and choose it like turners: the rope's sweep is the session's true size — walk the full arc the rope will travel, plus a falling body's length beyond it on every side, and clear or rule out everything inside that country. Test the rope yourself: turn it with another adult or an experienced teen and feel its arc — a rope that will not hold a steady, even rhythm at a kind height is replaced now, not cursed later, because every jumper in the session will be asked to trust this rope's timing with their feet. Set the session's shape in advance and write it on the slate: turning practice first, entries second, figures last, because a crew that starts with tricks has skipped the two skills the tricks stand on. Pair the first turners deliberately — your steadiest pair, not your loudest volunteers — since the whole room will calibrate its courage on the first rope they are offered, and a kind first rope makes brave jumpers of the cautious.

Materials

  • one long rope of honest length and weight (long enough for two turners standing well apart and three jumpers between them, heavy enough to keep a true arc — a clothesline-weight rope turns kindly; a flyaway cord teaches nobody timing)
  • a flat, clear turning ground agreed in advance (the crew's floor: no obstacles inside the rope's whole sweep plus a body's length on every side, checked by walking the sweep before the rope first turns)
  • the crew slate and chalk or marker (the session's record: who turned, who entered clean, which figures the crew landed — kept by the crew's own keeper at the rope's side)
  • a second, shorter rope for the practice corner (where entries are rehearsed at walking pace against a rope turned by hand, slowly, before any jumper meets the long rope at full turn)

Steps

  1. The framing, rope at rest across the ground: 'The rope belongs to the turners; the jumping belongs to everyone. In this crew the skilled job is not the jumping — it is the turning. Turners hold the height, keep the beat, read the jumper, and rescue a failing entry by lifting the rope over it instead of letting it trip: a good turner makes ordinary people look brave. Jumpers: your job is timing, and timing is learnable — you watch the rope, you enter on its far beat, you jump in the middle where the rope is honest, and you leave the way you came in while the rope is still turning. Nobody jumps alone today, nobody is counted out, and a miss is information, not a verdict: the slate records what the crew landed, and a jumper who misses twice changes jobs — to turning, to keeping, to the practice corner — and comes back when they choose, because a crew has many jobs and all of them are the session. We start with the rope turning empty, until its beat is in the room's feet. Then we jump.' The rope turns empty for a full minute while the crew claps its beat, so the first entry is made into a rhythm the whole room already holds.
  2. Turning school, the session's foundation, run before any entry at speed: pairs turn for one jumper-free minute each while the crew reads their rope aloud against the slate's three laws — height (the arc's top stays where it was promised, neither climbing to show off nor sagging to rest), beat (one turn, one beat, no hurrying a jumper by speeding and no rescuing by stopping), and hands (handles turning from the wrist, elbows quiet, the turners' eyes on the middle of the rope, never on their own hands). A pair whose rope drifts is coached by the crew in the open — turning is taught publicly because it is practised publicly — and every crew member turns before anyone is allowed to specialise in jumping, because a jumper who has never turned does not yet know what they are asking of the rope. The practice corner runs alongside for entries at walking pace: rope turned slow and low by hand, jumpers stepping in, standing the middle for three turns, and stepping out, until the geometry of entry is in their feet rather than their worries.
  3. Entries, the session's heart, built one jumper at a time: the crew forms its line at the rope's open side, the turners call the beat aloud until the room holds it, and each jumper enters on the far beat — in as the rope passes away, two settling jumps in the middle, out on the turners' call while the rope still turns. Turners rescue as the framing promised: a jumper arriving late is lifted over by the rope's height, a jumper tiring mid-entry is talked out on the beat — 'out on three' — never left to tangle and fall in front of the line. The slate records clean entries by name and rescues by name too, in the same chalk, because a rescued entry is a turner's achievement recorded at the jumper's line, and the crew reads both columns with the same pride at the close. Jumpers who want more take the harder doors the session offers in order: entering from the far side, turning a half-turn mid-entry, two jumpers entering on consecutive beats — each door named, demonstrated by its first taker, and never owed by anybody.
  4. Figures round, when the entries are steady and only then: the crew builds its figures from the skills the session has actually shown — jumpers in pairs entering together, a jumper crossing to exit the far side, three in the middle for a counted run of turns, the whole crew in for one heroic counted ten — each figure attempted by volunteers, spotted by the turners' rescue, and chalked on the slate landed or not-landed with the attempt's true number. A figure that fails three honest attempts is retired by the crew for the day, in the open, and its retirement is chalked like a landing, because knowing what today's crew cannot yet hold is a result the slate exists to carry. Between figures the rope rests and the turners rotate — turning is work, arms tire, and a tired turner's rope drops its height exactly when jumpers are bravest; the rotation is set by the slate before the round starts, so no pair turns a whole figures round on goodwill and fumes.
  5. Closing, the crew's count and the rope coiled by its turners: the slate is read aloud entire — turners by name, clean entries, rescues, figures landed and retired — and the session's one number is agreed and chalked large: the longest counted run the whole crew held in the middle together, whether it is forty turns or four, because that number, honestly counted, is the crew's mark to beat next session and nobody else's business. The tell-back is said with the rope coiled on the ground between the crew: 'A long rope is a promise two people keep with their wrists so that other people can be brave in the middle of it. Today the turners held the height, the jumpers trusted the beat, and every miss taught the crew something the slate now holds. Nobody jumped alone, nobody sat out a failure, and the rope never stopped being kind on purpose. Next session the mark on the slate is waiting. Crews are beaten by it or beat it — either way, it will be counted honestly.' The rope is inspected as it is coiled — fraying noted to the adults — and stored where the crew will find it with the slate.

Variations

Easier: A two-turner, one-jumper version (a small crew or a first session): the whole discipline at its smallest size — turning school, practice corner, entries, the slate — with figures saved for the session the entries hold steady.

Harder: A double-rope version (a crew whose single rope is truly steady): two ropes turned egg-beater style by four turners, entries rehearsed first against one rope turning, and the session's laws — rescue, rotation, honest counting — applied double, because the difficulty is.

⚠️ Safety

The rope's sweep is this record's first law and it is geographic: nobody stands, sits, crosses or leaves belongings inside the turning arc plus a body's length beyond it, except the jumpers whose entry the turners are expecting, and the line waits outside the sweep at the open side where the framing placed it. Turners hold handles with a grip that keeps the rope if it snags — never wrapped around a hand or wrist, because a snagged rope must be droppable, and a rope that catches a jumper is dropped by both turners on the adult's call or the turners' own judgement, instantly, without finishing the beat. Jumpers wear shoes that grip and stay on; loose laces are tied before the line, not during an entry. The practice corner is not optional for new jumpers and re-entry after a tangle is always through it — nobody returns to the long rope shaken and unbriefed, because the next entry after a fright is the session's most losable moment. The adult watches turners for the fatigue signs the rotation exists to prevent — dropping height, drifting beat — and ends or rests the rope on those signs as readily as on any fall; a fall inside the rope stops the session until the jumper is up, checked, and choosing for themselves whether the practice corner, another job, or the line is next.

🤝 Inclusion notes

Turning a long rope is a seated-tolerant, strength-sharing craft, and this crew staffs it as the honour it is: turners may turn sitting on a low bench at the rope's end where the ground allows, handles can be worked by a pair sharing one end in relay, and the slate-keeper — caller of the beat, recorder of entries and rescues, holder of the crew's mark — runs the session's voice from the rope's side at full authority. The beat is given in sight as well as sound: the lead turner raises a free shoulder on each turn for jumpers who read rhythm with their eyes, and entries are called by gesture as well as voice as the crew agrees at the framing, so no jumper's timing depends on hearing one voice across a turning rope. The practice corner runs at whatever pace its jumper sets, for as many rehearsals as they want, with no audience but its turner — a door into the session that never has to be explained to the line. Every job in the crew rotates through the slate's published order, so a member whose day is turning, keeping and coaching entries is not having a lesser session: the framing said the skilled job is the turning, and the crew's honours are chalked to prove it.
