Big One Statue, Many Bodies

The whole group builds ONE statue together, an arm here, a crouch there, then everybody freezes at once and the statue stands, breathing quietly.

Before you begin

Ages
3–3
Group
4–16 kids
Time
10–20 min
Where
Indoors & Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Draw the first statue card big and genuinely buildable at three: a star is five points, five children standing tall with arms up, close together, is a star a room can actually make; a 'dragon' is not a first statue, it is an argument with scales. Practise the freeze yourself, honestly: statues at three hold for a count of ten on a good day, and the session's timings are built on that truth. A statue asked to hold for thirty is a statue designed to melt, and melting gets blamed on the wrong people.

Materials

  • one big statue card, adult-drawn (a bold simple statue to aim at first, a star, a tree, a bridge, one card per session is plenty)
  • a clear statue ground (a rug or mat big enough for the whole group to stand on together)
  • no other materials. The group IS the material

Steps

  1. Spread the statue card where everyone can see it and say the plain job: 'A statue is usually ONE person standing very still. Ours is going to be ALL of us. Each person is one part of it, and it only stands when everybody is in it.' State the laws whole: 'We build it piece by piece, walking. When I say STATUE, everybody freezes, feet, hands, faces. Frozen people do not poke, lean on, or hold up anybody else. Your part holds YOU up; the statue holds together because everyone does their own part.' Build the star together first, piece by piece, you placing yourself as one of its points.
  2. First freeze together: pieces in place, the call, 'STATUE', and the group freezes for a count of five, counted by the adult out loud, slowly, like a small ceremony. At five: 'MELT', and everyone melts down into a cheerful puddle on the rug, shaking arms out. The melt is not the statue failing; it is the statue's second half, and it is taught in the same breath as the freeze, because a freeze with no kind ending is a trap, not a game. Name the achievement whole: 'Five people. One star. It stood.'
  3. Building stretch: the group designs its OWN statue now. The adult scribes, hands stay the materials. Ideas arrive as poses, tested one at a time: 'A bridge needs two tall sides and a top, who is the top?' (Answer, found by trying: two tall sides and a third person standing between them with arms stretched to touch both, a bridge with a person as its span, held by nobody.) Pieces are placed by the builders walking, negotiating in statue language: 'I'm the star's point. I need to be at the EDGE.' A statue that keeps falling gets redesigned by its own builders, kindly: changing the design is the engineering, and the record says so.
  4. Audience moment: once a statue stands for a full count of ten, it earns a viewing. Any other adult, class, or the adult's own slow walk around it, describing it like a museum guide at full respect: 'Note the star's tallest point. Note how the bridge's span connects BOTH sides.' Being LOOKED at, properly, while holding still together, is the deepest freeze of the session, and the guide's seriousness is what makes it delicious rather than silly.
  5. Finish with the group's favourite statue rebuilt one last time, frozen for its best count yet, melted with ceremony, and the tell-back naming the parts as a roster: 'The star had five points and every point was a person. The bridge stood because its sides stood. Nobody held anybody up, and the whole thing stood anyway. That is the trick this room now knows.'

Variations

Easier: A pairs version: two-person statues only (a table, one crouched, one flat-backed beside; a tree and its apple), the whole curriculum of freeze-together inside ten minutes for the smallest crews.

Harder: A silhouette version (bright day, big wall): the statue is built against a sunlit wall so the group can see its own shadow-shape standing. Building for the shadow is a second design problem laid over the first.

Safety

Every part holds ITSELF up: that law is the session's safety system, stated first and enforced gently throughout, no child leans their weight on, hangs from, climbs onto, or is held up by another child, ever, and statue designs that need it are redesigned on the spot by the adult acting as the group's safety engineer: 'A statue whose top needs holding is a statue asking for a different top.' Freezes are short (a count of five growing to ten) and melts are always available: any statue piece may melt themselves out at any moment, no reason needed, and the statue stands one part smaller with the room's respect intact. Building happens at walking pace on the clear rug; a fallen piece is helped up by the adult, checked, and offered a ground-level part if they want back in.

Inclusion notes

Every statue needs parts at every height, and the design round assigns them as honours, not accommodations: the star's base point, the bridge's grounded side, the tree's roots, a part performed from any seated or supported position is a load-bearing part of the design, discussed and placed like any other. A builder can also hold the architect's job for a whole statue: standing outside it with the adult, placing pieces by direction. 'You, tall, at the edge... you, crouched, in the middle', and calling STATUE and MELT for the group; the architect's statue is theirs in full, and the museum guide describes it by their name.
