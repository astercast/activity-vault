Big Zip Up-and-Down Board

One chunky zip on a sturdy board: grip the big pull, slide it up slow, slide it down slow — a coat's hardest job, practised where no coat is waiting.

Ages
3–3
Group
1–6 kids
Time
10–20 min
Where
Indoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Make or check the board like the tool it is: the zip must run SMOOTHLY — a zip that snags teaches yanking, so work it up and down twenty times yourself first, free its teeth of threads, and retire any zip that catches, because the board's whole lesson is that slow and steady is what works. The pull gets a ribbon loop a whole fist can hold. The fabric sits taut on the board so the zip's path never wrinkles under a pull; a slack path is a snag built in advance.

Materials

  • one zip board (adult-made: a chunky jacket zip sewn or firmly fixed on stiff fabric over board, the pull tab big enough for a whole fist — a ribbon loop through the pull helps)
  • a lap or low table to rest the board on

Steps

  1. Show the board closed and say the plain job: 'Coats have zips. Zips are a two-direction job: up joins the two sides together, down lets them apart. This board is where hands practise, so no coat ever has to wait on a zip again.' Work the zip once yourself, both ways, SLOWLY, letting the group hear the teeth join and part: the zip's sound is its own progress report.
  2. State the one rule: 'Slow pulls only. If it stops, your hand stops — a zip is never forced, because a forced zip is a broken zip.' First pull together: a kid's fist through the ribbon loop, your hand under theirs not pulling, just present, and the zip travels up at the pace the teeth allow. The stop-and-back-off moment gets rehearsed on purpose: meet a snag, stop, back half a tooth, go again — named out loud as the expert move it is.
  3. Up-and-down rounds: each worker takes the board on their lap or the low table and runs the zip its whole journey, up — pause at the top, the two sides one side now — and down, the sides parting like a road opening. The full trip is the unit: nobody rushes to finish, because the quiet in this activity is the sound of a job being done at the speed it asks for.
  4. Two-hand discovery, arriving on its own schedule: workers will find that one hand must hold the board's bottom still while the other pulls — the zip pulls the board otherwise. Name it when it lands: 'One hand holds, one hand works. That is every zip, on every coat, for everybody.' A board that teaches its own two-handedness has taught the dressing skill whole.
  5. Finish with the board zipped up and passed hand to hand for one last look at the joined seam — a straight, closed line made by slow hands. Boards live in the quiet corner after today, available at coat time's hardest moments and calm ones alike: a skill practised in quiet gets used in weather, which is the entire reason it exists.

Variations

Easier: Half-trips only: the adult starts every zip halfway, workers finish the up or the down; the journey is halved and the expert stop-and-back-off still gets its rehearsal.

Harder: A second board joins with its zip running the other way, or a bigger-toothed camping zip: workers must read a new zip's pace before pulling — same rule, new teeth, and the comparison is the lesson repeated with interest.

⚠️ Safety

Zips pinch: the rule is in the method — fingers live on the pull and its ribbon, never on the teeth's path, and a pull stops the instant anything (fabric, skin, thread) is in the track. The board is the only zip practised on: nobody practises on a worn coat, theirs or anyone's, where a pinch lands on a body instead of a board. Boards are adult-checked before every session — a detached pull or a tooth out of line retires the board until it is repaired, no exceptions negotiated by enthusiasm.

🤝 Inclusion notes

The zip works at lap height, table height, or held upright by the adult at exactly the angle its worker directs — the board travels, the skill doesn't change. A pull can be gripped by a fist through the ribbon, pushed with a palm heel along its track on a board laid flat, or directed — 'up... stop... down' — while a partner's hand pulls at the director's pace: the pacing, which is the skill's heart, belongs to the director in full.
