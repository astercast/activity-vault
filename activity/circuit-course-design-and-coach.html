Circuit Course, Designed and Coached

Teens design a circuit of stations, test every station on themselves, then coach it for their peers. Design is the workout before the workout.

Before you begin

Ages
13–14
Group
8–30 kids
Time
40–60 min
Where
Indoors & Outdoors
Prep
Some prep needed

Setup

Inventory before imagination: the design panel walks the room and lists what the circuit may honestly use, every mat, cone, rope and square metre of floor, and the adult rules the boundaries in the same walk: no maximal lifts, no heavy loads, nothing thrown at anybody, no station whose failure mode is a fall. The panel then drafts its long list of candidate stations against the course's shape, which is set now and never negotiated mid-session: stations in the high single digits, work intervals short enough that form survives the last one, a rest station designed in as a real station with a card of its own, and every station required to offer an easier door and a harder door on its card, so the course fits the room that arrives rather than the room the designers imagined. Each candidate station is written up provisionally, move, count, cue, and the ledger opens with the whole long list, because the testing round is about to kill a third of it and the dead deserve a record.

Materials

  • the station stock (the room's honest equipment: mats, cones, ropes, light balls, benches, inventoried by the designers before a single station is proposed, because a circuit designed for equipment that does not exist is fan fiction)
  • station cards and a marker per station (each card states the move, the count or time, the form cue in five words or fewer, and the easier and harder doors, written by the designers, readable at a glance by a tired athlete)
  • the course clock (one visible timer for the whole room. Work and rest are the circuit's heartbeat and everybody, athletes and coaches alike, runs on the same pulse)
  • the design ledger (proposals, test results, cuts and keeps with their reasons. The course's memory, so next term's designers inherit evidence instead of folklore)

Steps

  1. The framing, cards in hand, athletes and designers one group: 'A station earns its place by being doable well when tired. Today this course is designed, not dealt: every station on this floor was proposed by somebody here, tested on the designers' own bodies, and kept or cut by what the test showed, and the cuts are in the ledger with their reasons, because a course that hides its rejects teaches nothing. Coaches: your station, your cue, your count. You watch form the way referees watch lines, and a tired athlete's sloppy tenth rep is your information, not their failure. Athletes: the doors are yours. Every card has an easier way in and a harder way up, and taking either is the course working as designed. The only wrong door is the one taken to impress somebody. The clock is the heartbeat. When it speaks, we move. Form is the whole game; counts are just how we keep score of it.' The course map is walked once, station by station, by the whole group before the clock starts, so no athlete meets a station for the first time at full pulse.
  2. Testing round, designers only, run before the course ever opens: the panel works its own long list at full honesty, each station done at the card's count, at working pace, by every designer in turn, while the others watch with the ledger and the session's one merciless question: does the tenth rep look like the first? Stations fail the test in the open and for named reasons: the cue nobody could follow, the move whose form collapsed by rep six, the station that needed a spotter the course does not have, the one that was simply a punishment dressed as exercise. Failures are cut or rebuilt on the spot, a count lowered, a cue rewritten to five honest words, a move swapped for its own foundation, and every outcome is entered in the ledger in the same ink: kept, cut, rebuilt, and the evidence for each. A designer whose favourite station dies in testing is publicly thanked for the kill at the course's opening, because the panel's pride is the course's quality, not its authors' feelings.
  3. First run, coached at full ceremony: the clock starts, the room rotates, and each coach works their station the way the framing promised. Cue called before the count starts, eyes on form, the easier door offered by name to any athlete whose tenth rep is drifting, the harder door opened for any athlete whose form is bored. Coaches keep their station's tally in the ledger's margin as the run proceeds: how many took which door, where form broke, which cue landed and which one nobody heard. The run is data collection wearing a workout's clothes, and the coaches are told so, because a course that is never re-measured after its first run is a first draft with a fan club. The adult walks the whole course continuously and only ever intervenes on form that risks a body; every other correction belongs to the coaches, whose authority the run exists to build.
  4. Revision round, between runs, on the ledger's evidence: the panel reads the tallies aloud and rebuilds the course in the open, the station where everyone took the easier door gets its count re-set, the cue that landed gets promoted to the card in the words that actually worked, the station whose queue formed while others stood empty gets moved or split. Athletes are heard in this round by right, not by favour: one sentence each on the station they would keep exactly as it stands, entered verbatim where the panel asks for it, because the people the course is for are witnesses the design cannot afford to waste. The revised cards are rewritten clean, the ledger's revision page is signed by the panel, and the second run goes out under the new map, shorter if the evidence says shorter, so the room feels a course improve inside a single session, which is the design lesson no lecture delivers.
  5. Closing, the course is handed over: the final run ends on the clock, the panel reads the ledger's day entire, proposals, kills, tallies, revisions, and the course is named and dated on its map, credited to its designers, and filed for the next group that books the room. The tell-back is said at the rest station, where everyone is standing by then anyway: 'This morning this course was a list of guesses. It is a course now, because its designers tested it on themselves, killed what failed, listened to the people who ran it, and changed it in writing. Any one of you can now be handed a room, an inventory and an hour, and return a workout that fits the bodies that arrive. That is coaching, and it is mostly this: propose honestly, test on yourself, watch the tenth rep, and revise in the open.' Cards and ledger are stored together; a course with a good ledger gets re-run by other groups, whose tallies join the book behind the designers' pages.

Variations

Easier: A four-station version (a small room or a first panel): four stations designed, tested and coached entire inside half an hour, the whole discipline at a size where every designer can coach every station once.

Harder: A term-league version: the course is re-run weekly by the same group, the ledger accumulates tallies across the term, stations are promoted, relegated and retired by the evidence, and the term's final course is designed from a season of the book's own numbers.

Safety

Form is this record's safety system and it is structural: every station is tested on the designers before any athlete touches it, the adult rules the station list in advance, bodyweight and light equipment only, no maximal efforts, no loads overhead, nothing whose failure is a fall, and any station whose tenth rep cannot be done well is rebuilt or cut in the ledger, in the open, before the course opens. The rest station is a real station on the map with a card of its own, and taking it needs no permission, no explanation and no witness; an athlete who sits out a whole run on it has used the course exactly as designed. Spacing is walked at setup: stations far enough apart that a drifting athlete enters nobody's swing, floor checked dry, equipment checked whole. Water lives at the rest station, the clock's intervals are set for the room's actual fitness and the day's actual heat, and the adult's whistle stops the whole course instantly for any form that risks a body. A stoppage the coaches are taught to welcome as the system working, never as a verdict on their station.

Inclusion notes

Designing is this record's great leveller, and coaching is its second: the panel's jobs, inventory, card-writing, tally-keeping, cue-calling, the ledger's revision page, are full course authority exercised by members whose bodies may be sitting the run out entirely, and the rotation is drawn so design and coaching are honours, not consolations. Every station card carries its easier and harder doors by law, stated in the same size of writing, so an athlete's route through the course is a set of choices the course itself offers, never an exception negotiated in public. Cues can be called, shown on the card held high, or demonstrated by a partner at the station; the clock's changes are signalled by sound and by the coaches' raised hands together, so no athlete's round depends on hearing one voice in a loud room. The map is walked by the whole group before the clock starts, at walking pace, which means every athlete, including any whose movement is wheeled, aided or slow, has rehearsed their own route and their own doors before a single rep is counted.
